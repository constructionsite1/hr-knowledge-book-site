    Performance Management

    Chapter 3 · Performance Management

    What this chapter covers. The purpose and cycle of performance management; goal frameworks (KRAs, KPIs, OKRs, SMART goals); competencies and the “how” of performance; appraisal methods from MBO to the balanced scorecard, BARS and 360-degree feedback; rating scales, forced distribution and calibration; rater bias; feedback and coaching; managing under-performance in the Indian legal context; links to pay, development and talent decisions; how leading companies redesigned their systems; and performance management for different workforce groups.

    Why it matters for interviews. Performance management is among the most frequently discussed HR topics in interviews and group discussions. Panels test both mechanics (how calibration works, what BARS is) and judgement (whether to keep the bell curve, how to rate a high performer with poor behaviour).

    3.1What performance management is for

    In short

    Performance management is the continuous process of setting expectations, developing and evaluating performance, and using the results for decisions. The annual appraisal is only one event within it.

    Recall

    • Performance management vs performance appraisal
    • Purposes: strategic, administrative, developmental, documentation
    • Goal-setting theory
    • Feedback evidence (Kluger & DeNisi)

    Definition. Performance management (PM) is the continuous process of aligning individual work with organisational goals, setting expectations, observing and supporting performance, evaluating it, and using the evaluation for decisions about pay, development and careers.

    Performance management versus performance appraisal. A performance appraisal is a periodic evaluation event, usually annual, that produces a rating or assessment. Performance management is the whole system around it: goal setting, ongoing feedback, coaching, review, and the decisions that follow. Many failures attributed to “performance management” are really failures of an appraisal-only approach, in which nothing happens between one annual form and the next.

    Purposes

    Purpose What PM provides Example decision
    StrategicTranslation of company strategy into team and individual goalsEnsuring a plant’s safety and cost goals appear in every supervisor’s objectives
    AdministrativeEvidence for pay, promotion, retention and exit decisionsIncrement through the merit matrix; promotion to the next grade
    DevelopmentalFeedback, identification of strengths and gaps, development plansCoaching, training, stretch assignments
    DocumentationRecords of expectations and performanceEvidence in a dispute over termination or promotion

    The purposes conflict. An employee who knows that a conversation will determine their bonus is less likely to discuss weaknesses openly, which undermines the developmental purpose. This tension explains why many organisations separate development conversations (frequent, informal) from evaluation decisions (periodic, calibrated).

    First principles: why PM can improve performance

    • Goal-setting theory (Locke and Latham): specific and challenging goals lead to higher performance than vague goals such as “do your best”, provided people are committed to the goals, have the ability to achieve them and receive feedback on progress. This is the strongest-evidenced theory in the field and the basis for SMART goals and OKRs.
    • Feedback research (Kluger and DeNisi, 1996, a review of over 600 studies): feedback improves performance on average, but in more than a third of cases it reduced performance, particularly when it focused attention on the person (“you are careless”) rather than on the task (“this report missed two data sources”). Well-designed PM therefore emphasises task-focused, specific and timely feedback.
    • Expectancy theory (Vroom): people are motivated when they believe effort leads to performance, performance leads to rewards, and they value those rewards. A PM system whose ratings do not visibly affect rewards weakens the second link.

    3.2The performance management cycle

    In short

    PM runs as a yearly cycle of four stages: plan, monitor and coach, review and calibrate, reward and develop. In most Indian companies it follows the April–March financial year.

    Recall

    • Plan → monitor → review → reward/develop
    • Mid-year review
    • Roles: employee, manager, HR
    • Three pillars: goals, actions, consequences
    Stage Timing (typical Indian cycle) Activities HR’s role
    1. PlanApril–MayCascade company goals to functions, teams and individuals; agree KRAs, KPIs or OKRs with weights; agree competency expectations and development goalsTemplates, goal-quality checks, alignment reviews
    2. Monitor and coachThroughout the year; formal mid-year review around OctoberRegular check-ins, feedback, coaching, adjusting goals when business conditions changeManager training; nudges and reminders; tracking check-in completion
    3. Review and calibrateMarch–AprilSelf-assessment, manager assessment, calibration across teams, final ratingsFacilitating calibration, checking distributions and bias
    4. Reward and developMay–JulyIncrements and bonuses, promotions, development plans, performance improvement plansApplying the merit matrix and bonus rules; linking to L&D and talent reviews

    Roles. The employee owns their goals and development and seeks feedback. The manager sets expectations, coaches, evaluates and explains decisions; the quality of PM depends more on managers than on any form or software. HR designs the system, trains managers, runs calibration, checks fairness and connects outcomes to reward and talent processes.

    The three pillars named in the compendium remain a useful test of any system: clear and measurable goals and competencies, concrete actions to achieve them, and consequences that follow performance. When consequences are missing, PM becomes paperwork.

    3.3Goal frameworks: KRA, KPI, OKR and SMART

    In short

    KRAs define areas of responsibility, KPIs measure them, and OKRs set a few ambitious objectives with measurable key results. SMART is a quality test for any goal.

    Recall

    • KRA = area; KPI = measure
    • OKR: objective + 3–5 key results
    • OKRs: stretch, often not tied to pay
    • Origin: Andy Grove (Intel), John Doerr (Google)
    • SMART
    • Cascading and weights

    Definitions

    Key result area (KRA). A broad area of responsibility in which a role is expected to deliver results, such as “workforce stability” or “statutory compliance”. The term is widely used in Indian organisations.

    Key performance indicator (KPI). A quantifiable measure of performance in a KRA, with a target. For the KRA “workforce stability”, a KPI could be “monthly voluntary attrition of operators below 1.5%”.

    Objectives and key results (OKR). A goal framework consisting of an objective, a qualitative statement of what is to be achieved, and three to five key results, measurable outcomes that show whether the objective has been met. OKRs were developed by Andy Grove at Intel in the 1970s and introduced to Google by John Doerr in 1999.

    Comparison

    Aspect KRA and KPI OKR
    PurposeMonitor ongoing performance of the roleDrive a few priority changes or improvements
    AmbitionTargets expected to be metStretch goals; achieving about 70% is often considered success
    ScopeCovers the whole roleCovers selected priorities only
    Time frameUsually annualOften quarterly, with annual objectives
    Link to payUsually directOften deliberately separated, so people set ambitious goals
    TransparencyUsually between employee and managerOften public across the organisation

    Example for a plant HR manager.

    Framework Example
    KRAWorkforce stability
    KPIMonthly operator attrition below 1.5%; new-joiner retention at 90 days above 85%
    OKRObjective: Make Plant 2 a place operators choose to stay. KR1: reduce monthly attrition from 2.4% to 1.5%. KR2: raise 90-day retention from 70% to 85%. KR3: raise the canteen and transport satisfaction score to at least 4 out of 5.

    Common mistakeOKRs do not replace KPIs. KPIs track the ongoing health of the business; OKRs focus effort on a few changes. Organisations that use OKRs still monitor KPIs.

    SMART goals

    A goal should be Specific (clear about what is to be achieved), Measurable (progress can be tracked), Achievable (stretching but realistic), Relevant (aligned with team and company priorities) and Time-bound (has a deadline). SMART is the practical application of goal-setting theory.

    Weak goal SMART version
    Improve hiringReduce average time-to-fill for engineering roles from 65 to 45 days by 31 December, without lowering the 90-day retention rate
    Work on employee engagementRaise the engagement score of the dispatch team from 58 to 65 in the next annual survey through monthly team meetings and a fixed-shift roster

    Cascading, alignment and weights

    Goals are usually cascaded from company to function, team and individual, so that each person can see how their work contributes. Pure top-down cascading is slow and can produce goals the employee does not own; many organisations now combine it with bottom-up goals proposed by employees, as in MBO. Each goal carries a weight (for example, 30% cost, 30% safety, 20% quality, 20% people development) so that the overall evaluation reflects priorities. Best practice is 4–6 goals per person, not 15.

    3.4Competencies and the “how” of performance

    In short

    Results show what was achieved; competencies and values show how. Modern systems assess both, often on two separate axes.

    Recall

    • What (results) vs how (behaviours)
    • Competency framework with proficiency levels
    • Two-axis rating
    • “Brilliant jerk” problem

    A competency is an observable combination of knowledge, skills, abilities and behaviours needed for effective performance in a role, defined at different proficiency levels (Chapter 4). In PM, competencies describe the expected behaviours: for example, “Stakeholder management: builds agreement between plant and corporate functions on conflicting priorities”.

    Assessing only results rewards people who achieve targets in damaging ways: by cutting safety corners, mistreating teams or hoarding information. Assessing only behaviours rewards people who are pleasant but do not deliver. Many organisations therefore rate the what (results against goals) and the how (competencies and values) separately, and combine them through a matrix.

    How: below expectations How: meets How: role model
    What: exceedsNot eligible for top rating or promotion until behaviour changesStrong performerTop performer
    What: meetsDevelopment need on behaviourSolid performerStrong performer
    What: belowPerformance improvement planDevelopment need on resultsDevelopment need on results

    Why it matters. What leaders reward defines the real culture (Schein, Chapter 7). If a high-revenue salesperson who bullies colleagues receives the top rating, every employee learns that results excuse behaviour.

    3.5Appraisal methods

    In short

    Each appraisal method was designed to solve a specific problem. Choose by purpose: measurable results (MBO), strategy translation (BSC), behaviour accuracy (BARS), development (360), talent decisions (9-box).

    Recall

    • MBO (Drucker, 1954)
    • BSC (Kaplan & Norton, 1992): 4 perspectives
    • BARS
    • 360-degree feedback
    • Forced distribution
    • Graphic rating, critical incident, ranking

    Overview

    Method How it works Best for Main weakness
    Graphic rating scaleRate traits or competencies on a numeric scale (e.g., 1–5)Simple, widely usedVague anchors; open to bias
    Ranking and paired comparisonOrder employees from best to worst, or compare every pairSmall groupsShows order only; unworkable for large teams
    Critical incidentRecord specific examples of effective and ineffective behaviour during the yearEvidence for feedbackTime-consuming; selective recording
    Management by objectives (MBO)Agree objectives jointly; evaluate achievementRoles with measurable outcomesIgnores how results were achieved; can encourage easy targets
    Balanced scorecard (BSC)Goals across financial, customer, internal process and learning perspectivesLeadership and unit goals linked to strategyComplex; poorly chosen measures distort behaviour
    BARSRating scale anchored to specific observed behavioursFrontline, service and supervisory rolesCostly to build; role-specific
    360-degree feedbackRatings from manager, peers, direct reports, sometimes customersDevelopment of managers and leadersPopularity effects; confidentiality concerns; risky for pay decisions
    Forced distributionFixed percentages of employees in each ratingPreventing rating inflation in large populationsAssumes all teams are normally distributed; damages collaboration

    Management by objectives (MBO)

    Introduced by Peter Drucker in The Practice of Management (1954). The manager and employee agree specific objectives, the employee chooses how to achieve them, progress is reviewed periodically, and evaluation is based on achievement. Its strength is participation and clarity: people commit more to goals they helped set. Example: an SEO manager and marketing head agree to “increase organic traffic by 20% by the end of the third quarter”; the manager chooses the tactics and reports monthly; the year-end evaluation compares actual growth with 20%. Intel’s use of MBO, refined by Andy Grove, evolved into OKRs.

    Balanced scorecard (BSC)

    Developed by Robert Kaplan and David Norton (Harvard Business Review, 1992) to address the limitation that financial measures describe the past but not the drivers of future performance. It measures performance from four linked perspectives:

    Perspective Question Example measures
    FinancialHow do we look to shareholders?Revenue growth, operating margin, return on capital
    CustomerHow do customers see us?Satisfaction, retention, on-time delivery
    Internal processWhat must we excel at?Cycle time, defect rate, turnaround time
    Learning and growthCan we continue to improve?Employee skills, engagement, retention, information systems

    The perspectives form a cause-and-effect chain: trained and engaged employees (learning) improve processes (internal), which satisfy customers (customer), which drives financial results. Example from the compendium: an airline trained ground crews to cut aircraft turnaround time, which improved on-time departures, which raised passenger loyalty, which increased revenue. In HR, the BSC is used to set leadership and unit goals and to build HR’s own scorecard.

    Behaviourally anchored rating scales (BARS)

    BARS replace vague scale points with descriptions of actual behaviour. Building one involves collecting critical incidents from experienced staff, grouping them into performance dimensions, and assigning each incident to a scale point.

    Rating “Handles customer complaints” (customer service executive)
    5Resolves the complaint at first contact, follows up within 24 hours, and records the root cause so it can be prevented
    4Resolves most complaints at first contact and escalates complex ones with complete information
    3Resolves routine complaints; escalates others correctly but sometimes without full details
    2Often needs supervisor help with routine complaints; follow-up is inconsistent
    1Argues with customers or fails to record complaints

    BARS improve consistency between raters and give clear feedback, because the employee can see exactly what a higher rating looks like. Their cost limits them to roles with many incumbents.

    360-degree feedback

    Feedback is collected from several sources: manager, peers, direct reports, the employee themselves, and sometimes internal or external customers. It is most valuable for leadership development, because direct reports and peers see behaviours that managers do not. Best practice: use it for development rather than pay; guarantee anonymity with a minimum number of raters per group (often three); focus questions on observable behaviours; and give the results through a trained coach with a development plan. Its weaknesses are rater fatigue, friendship or rivalry effects, large amounts of data, and confidentiality concerns, particularly in high power-distance cultures where criticising a manager feels risky (Chapter 7).

    The 9-box grid

    The 9-box grid is a talent-review tool rather than an appraisal method. It places employees on two axes, performance (current results) and potential (likely ability to succeed in larger roles), each at three levels.

    Low performance Moderate performance High performance
    High potentialRough diamond: possibly wrong role or new to itGrowth employeeStar: accelerate, include on succession slates
    Moderate potentialInconsistent performerCore contributorHigh performer: stretch assignments
    Low potentialUnder-performer: improvement plan or exitEffective in current roleTrusted professional: retain and reward as an expert

    It is used in succession planning and to decide where to invest development. Its weaknesses are that potential is hard to judge and prone to bias (managers tend to see potential in people similar to themselves) and that it depends on the quality of performance ratings.

    CorrectionThe compendium (p. 37) says McKinsey developed the 9-box grid in the 1970s to assess employees. The original was the GE–McKinsey nine-box matrix for evaluating business portfolios; HR later adapted the format for talent reviews. The claim that Google has used the 9-box since the early 2000s is not supported by a public source and is best avoided.

    3.6Rating scales, distributions and calibration

    In short

    Ratings must be consistent across managers to be fair. Forced distribution imposes consistency crudely; calibration achieves it through discussion and evidence.

    Recall

    • 3-, 4- and 5-point scales
    • Forced vs guided distribution
    • Normalisation
    • Calibration steps
    • GE vitality curve (20-70-10)

    Rating scales

    Scale Example labels Advantage Disadvantage
    5-pointOutstanding, exceeds, meets, partly meets, does not meetDifferentiates wellRatings cluster at 3 and 4
    4-pointNo middle pointForces a judgement above or below “meets”Can feel harsh
    3-pointExceeds, meets, belowSimple; fewer argumentsLittle differentiation for pay

    Forced distribution

    Under forced distribution (the “bell curve”), a fixed percentage of employees must receive each rating, such as 10% top, 70% middle and 20% bottom. Jack Welch popularised it at General Electric as the vitality curve (top 20%, vital 70%, bottom 10%), with the bottom 10% often exited, a practice known as “rank and yank”.

    Arguments for Arguments against
    Prevents rating inflation, where nearly everyone is rated above averagePerformance in real teams is not normally distributed; strong teams are penalised
    Forces managers to differentiate, which pay-for-performance requiresEncourages competition with colleagues instead of collaboration
    Identifies persistent low performersManagers game it, for example by rotating low ratings
    Demotivates solid performers labelled as “average”

    Microsoft dropped stack ranking in 2013, and GE, Accenture and Infosys moved away from forced curves around 2015–16. Many organisations now use guided distribution: a recommended distribution applied at the level of a large population (a function or business), not imposed on each small team, and adjusted through calibration.

    Normalisation is the Indian term for adjusting ratings across teams to fit a distribution or a budget; when done mechanically, it is a frequent cause of employee distrust.

    Calibration

    Calibration is a meeting in which managers review proposed ratings together so that the same standards apply across teams.

    1. Managers submit proposed ratings with evidence (results against goals, examples of behaviour).
    2. HR prepares data: proposed distribution by team, gender, location and tenure; prior ratings; outliers.
    3. Managers present their top and bottom ratings and borderline cases; peers ask questions and challenge.
    4. Ratings are adjusted where standards differ, and the reasons are recorded.
    5. HR checks the final distribution for patterns that suggest bias.
    6. Managers communicate final ratings to employees and explain the reasoning.

    Benefits: consistency, reduced leniency, shared standards, better data for pay decisions. Risks: the most vocal manager’s view dominates; employees not known to other managers are disadvantaged. HR’s role is to facilitate with evidence and to watch for these effects.

    3.7Rating errors and bias

    In short

    Ratings reflect the rater as much as the ratee. Structured evidence, calibration and data checks reduce the main errors.

    Recall

    • Halo, horn, recency, leniency, strictness, central tendency
    • Similar-to-me, contrast, proximity bias
    • Idiosyncratic rater effect (~62%)

    Research on ratings has found that a large share of the variation in performance ratings reflects the individual rater’s tendencies rather than the performance of the person rated. A study by Scullen, Mount and Goff (2000), cited in Deloitte’s redesign, attributed about 62% of rating variance to such “idiosyncratic rater effects”.

    Error Description Example
    HaloOne positive trait influences the whole ratingFluent presenter rated high on all competencies
    HornOne negative trait influences the whole ratingQuiet employee assumed to lack initiative
    RecencyRecent events outweigh the full periodRating based on the last two months
    Leniency / strictnessRater consistently rates high or lowOne manager gives everyone “exceeds”
    Central tendencyRater avoids extremesEveryone rated “meets” to avoid difficult conversations
    Similar-to-me (affinity)Higher ratings for people like the raterSame college, background or working style
    ContrastRating depends on comparison with others rated just beforeAverage performer looks weak next to a star
    Proximity biasEmployees seen more often in person are rated higherHybrid workers rated below office-based colleagues

    Controls. Specific goals and behaviourally anchored criteria; evidence gathered through the year (check-in notes, critical incidents); calibration; rater training; and analysis of rating outcomes by gender, location, work arrangement and manager to detect patterns.

    3.8Feedback, check-ins and coaching

    In short

    Frequent, specific, task-focused feedback improves performance; vague or personal feedback can reduce it. Regular check-ins are the core of modern PM.

    Recall

    • SBI model (CCL)
    • Feedforward (Goldsmith)
    • Check-ins
    • Coaching questions (GROW)
    • Manager capability

    Principles of effective feedback, drawn from the research in Section 3.1: it is specific (refers to particular actions and results), timely (close to the event), task-focused (about the work, not the person’s character), balanced (recognises what works as well as what does not) and forward-looking (what to do next).

    The SBI model (Center for Creative Leadership) structures feedback in three parts: Situation (when and where), Behaviour (what the person did, observably), Impact (the effect on results or others).

    Example: “In Tuesday’s union meeting (situation), you summarised management’s proposal before responding to the demands (behaviour). That lowered the tension and got us an agreement on the shift roster (impact).”

    Feedforward (Marshall Goldsmith) focuses on suggestions for the future rather than criticism of the past, which reduces defensiveness.

    Check-ins are short, frequent conversations (monthly or quarterly) about priorities, progress, obstacles and development. They do not replace evaluation but make it more accurate and less surprising. Coaching conversations often follow the GROW model: Goal (what the person wants to achieve), Reality (the current situation), Options (possible actions), Will (what they commit to doing).

    Manager capability is the constraint. Every redesign that replaced annual reviews with check-ins depended on managers being willing and able to hold good conversations. Organisations that changed the process without training managers found that check-ins simply did not happen.

    3.9Managing under-performance

    In short

    Diagnose the cause first, then use a structured improvement plan with support and documentation. In India, the process must respect standing orders, contracts and natural justice.

    Recall

    • Ability, motivation, environment
    • IDP vs PIP
    • PIP: 60–90 days, measurable, supported
    • Standing orders; natural justice
    • Non-disciplinary termination of a workman may count as retrenchment

    Diagnosis

    Under-performance has three broad causes, and each needs a different response.

    Cause Indicators Response
    Ability (knowledge, skills)New to the role; tasks beyond current skillTraining, coaching, job aids, closer supervision
    MotivationCapable but disengaged; recent change in attitudeConversation about causes; goals and consequences; personal or career issues
    EnvironmentUnclear expectations, missing tools, poor processes, conflicting prioritiesFix the work system (see Mager and Pipe, Chapter 4)

    IDP and PIP

    Individual development plan (IDP) Performance improvement plan (PIP)
    ForAny employee, especially high-potentialsEmployees below the expected standard
    GoalGrowth towards future rolesReaching the required standard in the current role
    ContentDevelopment goals; experiences, coaching and training (70-20-10); timelineSpecific performance gaps; measurable targets; support provided; review dates; consequences
    DurationOngoingUsually 60–90 days

    A fair PIP states the gap with evidence, sets measurable targets that are achievable within the period, provides real support (coaching, training, resources), holds regular reviews with written notes, and states the consequences clearly. A PIP used only as a formality before a decided exit damages trust and the employer brand: Indian employees discuss “PIP culture” openly on professional networks and review sites.

    India context

    The legal position depends on the type of employee.

    • Workers covered by standing orders (industrial establishments; the IR Code threshold for standing orders is now 300 workers, with model standing orders applying more widely in practice): termination for misconduct requires the disciplinary process in the standing orders, including a charge-sheet and a domestic enquiry following natural justice (Chapter 10). Poor performance is not itself misconduct unless the standing orders define it so (for example, habitual negligence).
    • A non-disciplinary termination of a worker, such as discharge for unsatisfactory performance, may be treated as retrenchment, which requires notice, retrenchment compensation and the reskilling fund contribution (Chapter 10).
    • Managerial and supervisory staff outside the definition of “worker” are mainly governed by their employment contracts. A documented PIP is still important as evidence that the decision was fair and not arbitrary if it is challenged.

    3.10Linking performance to pay, development and talent

    In short

    Performance outcomes feed three separate decisions: rewards (increments, bonuses), development (IDPs, training) and talent (promotions, succession). Each needs different information.

    Recall

    • Merit matrix
    • Bonus multiplier
    • Promotion criteria: performance + potential + readiness
    • Talent review and 9-box
    Decision Inputs from PM Process
    IncrementsYear-end ratingMerit matrix of rating and compa-ratio (Chapter 2, Section 2.8)
    BonusesRating and goal achievementBonus pool funded by company results; individual multiplier by rating (Chapter 2, Section 2.20)
    DevelopmentStrengths and gaps, competency assessment, career aspirationsIDP; training needs analysis (Chapter 4)
    PromotionSustained performance, potential, readiness for the larger rolePromotion panel or assessment; job availability in the structure
    Succession and talentPerformance and potential over timeTalent review using the 9-box; succession slates for critical roles (Chapter 6)

    A common error is to treat promotion as a reward for performance in the current role. Performance shows that someone is effective in their present job, but promotion should depend on evidence that they can succeed in the next one. Promoting the best salesperson to sales manager without assessing leadership potential is a classic example.

    3.11How organisations have redesigned PM

    In short

    From about 2012, many large companies replaced annual ratings and forced curves with frequent check-ins. Several later reintroduced simpler ratings for pay decisions. The lasting change is more frequent conversation, not the absence of ratings.

    Recall

    • Adobe Check-in (2012)
    • Microsoft (2013)
    • Deloitte snapshot (2015)
    • Accenture, GE (2015–16)
    • Infosys (2016)
    • Google GRAD (2022)
    Organisation Previous system Change Lesson
    Adobe (2012)Annual reviews and ratings consuming about 80,000 manager hours a yearCheck-ins between managers and employees at least quarterly; no ratings; managers allocate increases within a budgetFrequent conversations improved clarity; voluntary attrition fell
    Microsoft (2013)Stack ranking against colleaguesDropped stack ranking; evaluation includes impact on others’ successRanking had discouraged collaboration
    Deloitte (2015)Annual objectives, single rating, lengthy consensus meetings, about 2 million hours a yearQuarterly or per-project performance snapshots: team leaders answer four questions about their own intended actions toward the person (for example, whether they would always want the person on their team, and whether the person is ready for promotion)Asking raters about their own intentions reduces idiosyncratic rater effects
    Accenture (2015–16)Annual review with rankingsDropped rankings and annual reviews in favour of ongoing feedbackRemoving rankings supported collaboration
    GE (2015–16)Vitality curveContinuous feedback through the PD@GE appEven the originator of forced ranking abandoned it
    Infosys (2016)Bell curveRemoved forced distribution; more frequent feedbackIndian IT followed global practice
    Google (2022)Twice-yearly ratingsGRAD (Googler Reviews and Development): one annual rating with regular development check-insSimplified ratings remain useful for pay decisions

    What happened next. Organisations without visible ratings found that pay decisions became harder to explain, and that managers still effectively rated people in private. Many therefore kept or reintroduced a simple year-end rating, while retaining frequent check-ins. The durable lesson is that one annual event cannot serve all of PM’s purposes at once; effective systems separate frequent development conversations from periodic, calibrated evaluation and from talent reviews.

    Technology and AI. PM platforms (SAP SuccessFactors, Workday, Darwinbox, Keka) support goal tracking, check-ins and continuous feedback. AI tools are being used to summarise feedback, draft reviews and flag rating patterns. They can improve evidence and detect bias, but using AI to decide ratings raises fairness, transparency and data-protection concerns (Chapter 9).

    3.12Performance management for different workforces

    In short

    One design does not fit every group. Plants, sales, project-based knowledge work, hybrid teams and gig workers need different measures and processes.

    Recall

    • Unionised workmen: settlements, team incentives
    • Sales: objective metrics
    • Project work: per-project feedback
    • Hybrid: outcomes, proximity bias
    • Gig: algorithmic ratings
    Workforce Typical approach Design considerations
    Unionised shop-floor workersPay set by long-term wage settlements; team or line-level productivity incentives (output, quality, safety, attendance); skill-based progression through multi-skillingIndividual ratings are rarely used and can be resisted by unions; incentive schemes are negotiated
    SalesObjective metrics (revenue, margin, collections, new accounts) with commission or incentive plansBalance volume with margin and customer quality; avoid targets that encourage mis-selling
    Project-based knowledge work (consulting, IT services)Feedback at the end of each project; aggregated evaluationEmployees work for several managers; the people manager collects inputs
    Hybrid and remote teamsOutcome-based goals, scheduled check-insGuard against proximity bias; define availability norms
    Gig and platform workersCustomer ratings and algorithmic metrics (acceptance rate, delivery time)Transparency of metrics, appeal against deactivation; state laws such as Karnataka’s 2025 gig-worker Act address these issues

    3.14Interview questions

    In short

    For opinion questions, state a position, give reasons, acknowledge the strongest counter-argument and finish with a practical design.

    Should companies use a bell curve?

    Not as a quota imposed on every team. Real teams are not normally distributed, forced quotas encourage gaming and competition with colleagues, and companies including Microsoft, GE, Accenture and Infosys moved away from them. The legitimate concern behind the bell curve is rating inflation, which makes differentiation impossible. A guided distribution applied across a large population, combined with evidence-based calibration, addresses that without forcing every small team into the same shape.

    What is the difference between a KPI and an OKR? Which would you use for an HR business partner?

    KPIs measure the ongoing performance of a role against expected targets; OKRs set a small number of ambitious objectives with measurable key results to drive change, and are often kept separate from pay. An HR business partner would have KPIs for continuing responsibilities such as attrition, time-to-fill and engagement, and one or two OKRs for change priorities, such as building first-line manager capability in a plant.

    Your top performer achieves every target but demoralises the team. How would you rate them?

    Rate results and behaviour separately. On results the person exceeds expectations; on behaviour they are below. They should not receive the top overall rating or a promotion until the behaviour changes, and they should receive specific feedback, coaching and possibly a 360 to provide evidence. Rewarding results regardless of behaviour signals to everyone what the organisation really values.

    What is calibration, and what can go wrong in it?

    Calibration is a meeting in which managers review proposed ratings together against shared standards and evidence, so that ratings are consistent across teams. It can go wrong when the most assertive manager dominates, when employees unknown to other managers are disadvantaged, or when it becomes mechanical normalisation to a curve. HR should facilitate with data on distributions and potential bias.

    How would you design performance management for unionised workers in a cement plant?

    Individual ratings are generally unsuitable because wages are set through long-term settlements and unions resist individual differentiation. I would use team or line-level productivity incentives linked to output, quality, safety and attendance, negotiated with the union; a skills matrix with pay progression for multi-skilling; and recognition schemes. Individual reviews would apply to officers and supervisors.

    The CEO wants to remove performance ratings entirely. How would you respond?

    Ask what problem the change is meant to solve: time spent, demotivation, or bias. Point out that companies that removed ratings still needed a way to make and explain pay decisions, and several reintroduced simple ratings. Propose a pilot in one business unit with frequent check-ins and calibrated manager discretion for pay, invest in manager training first, and define success measures such as check-in frequency, engagement items on feedback and regretted attrition.
