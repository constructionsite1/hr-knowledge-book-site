    Pay Structures: From Job to Salary

    Chapter 2 · Compensation and Benefits · Part 1 of 3

    What this part covers. How organisations decide what to pay for each job and each person: the principles behind pay decisions, job analysis, job evaluation, grading, salary benchmarking, pay ranges, the measures used to position individuals within a range, and how annual increments are allocated. Part 2 covers how pay is packaged in India (CTC, statutory components, the wage-code definition of wages). Part 3 covers variable pay, long-term incentives and benefits.

    Why it matters for interviews. Compensation questions are among the most frequently asked in HR interviews, and most of them test whether you understand this sequence and the reasoning behind it, rather than individual definitions.

    2.1The two principles behind every pay decision

    In short

    Pay decisions must be defensible on two grounds: fairness between jobs inside the company (internal equity) and competitiveness with the outside market (external competitiveness). Each technique in this chapter serves one of the two.

    Recall

    • Internal equity
    • External competitiveness
    • Individual equity
    • Equity theory (Adams)
    • Determinants: job, market, person, organisation

    Internal equity means that jobs of similar size and value to the organisation are paid similarly, and larger jobs are paid more than smaller ones. It answers the question: is this job paid fairly relative to other jobs in the same organisation? It is achieved mainly through job analysis, job evaluation and grading (Sections 2.2 to 2.4).

    External competitiveness means that pay is set at a deliberate level relative to what other employers pay for comparable jobs. It answers the question: can we attract and retain people for this job at this pay? It is achieved through salary benchmarking and a chosen pay positioning (Section 2.5).

    A third principle, individual equity, concerns people in the same job: differences in their pay should be explained by legitimate factors such as performance, experience and time in role, rather than by negotiation skill, bias or the timing of hiring. It is managed through pay ranges, compa-ratios and the merit matrix (Sections 2.6 to 2.8).

    Why these principles matter: the first-principles view. Employees judge pay mainly by comparison. J. Stacy Adams’s equity theory (1965) describes how people compare the ratio of what they contribute to what they receive with the same ratio for others. When they perceive inequity, they act to restore balance: they reduce effort, ask for more, or leave. This is why a pay system’s credibility depends less on how much it pays and more on whether its decisions can be explained.

    Factors that determine pay

    Pay for a given person is the result of four groups of factors, which act at different levels.

    Group Factors Where it enters the pay system
    The jobSize and complexity of the role, accountability, required knowledge, criticality to the business, working conditionsJob evaluation and grade
    The marketPay for comparable jobs in the relevant industry and location, scarcity of the skill, labour supply and demand, inflationBenchmarking and range midpoints
    The personPerformance, experience and proficiency in the role, potential, scarcity of their particular skillsPosition within the range; increments; bonuses
    The organisation and the lawAbility to pay, pay philosophy (lead, match or lag the market), budgets, minimum wages and floor wages, the statutory definition of wages, collective agreements with unionsPositioning decisions, budgets, structure design and compliance

    The table explains a common confusion. Two people can be paid differently for valid reasons operating at different levels: their jobs are in different grades (job), their jobs are benchmarked to different markets (market), or they sit in different parts of the same range (person).

    Interview angle“What determines an employee’s salary?” is best answered with these four groups and one example of each. Mentioning that the factors act at different stages of the system (grade, range, position in range) shows structured understanding.

    2.2Job analysis

    In short

    Job analysis collects systematic information about what a job involves. Its outputs, the job description and job specification, are the raw material for job evaluation, benchmarking, recruitment and performance management.

    Recall

    • Job description vs job specification
    • Methods: interview, questionnaire, observation, SME workshop, work diary, critical incidents
    • Analyse the job, not the jobholder
    • Title rationalisation

    Definition. Job analysis is the systematic collection and recording of information about a job: its purpose, responsibilities, decisions, relationships, working conditions, and the knowledge, skills and experience required to perform it.

    Principle: analyse the job, not the person. A pay structure is built around jobs because jobs are relatively stable, whereas jobholders change. If pay is set around individuals, the structure reflects past negotiations rather than the work, and becomes impossible to explain or maintain.

    Outputs.

    Output Describes Contents Main uses
    Job description (JD)The jobJob title, purpose, reporting line, key responsibilities and result areas, scope (budget, team size), key relationshipsJob evaluation, benchmarking, performance goals, onboarding
    Job specificationThe person requiredQualifications, experience, technical and behavioural competencies, any physical or legal requirementsRecruitment and selection, training needs analysis

    Methods. Most organisations combine several methods, because each has blind spots.

    Method How it works Best for Limitation
    InterviewsStructured conversations with jobholders and managersManagerial and professional rolesTime-consuming; jobholders may inflate their role
    Structured questionnairesStandard questions answered by many jobholders (e.g., the Position Analysis Questionnaire)Large populations; comparable data across jobsDepends on respondents’ understanding
    ObservationAnalyst watches the work being doneOperational and shop-floor jobsMisses cognitive work and rare tasks
    Subject-matter-expert workshopsExperienced people define a job family togetherBuilding standard JDs and job familiesCan reflect the most senior view
    Work diaries / logsJobholder records activities over a periodVaried or unpredictable rolesBurden on jobholder
    Critical incident techniqueCollects examples of very effective and very ineffective behaviourDefining competencies and performance standardsDoes not describe routine work

    Common problems and best practice. Job analysis often reveals title inflation and title proliferation: one title used for very different jobs, or many titles for the same job. In a company of 1,400 employees it is not unusual to find over 200 titles. Best practice is to standardise titles against a defined set of job families and levels (Chapter 6, job architecture), write JDs in a common template focused on outcomes rather than task lists, and have them validated by both jobholders and managers.

    2.3Job evaluation

    In short

    Job evaluation ranks jobs by relative size within one organisation. It produces internal equity; it does not evaluate people and does not set pay on its own.

    Recall

    • Non-analytical: ranking, classification
    • Analytical: factor comparison, point-factor
    • Hay: Know-how, Problem solving, Accountability
    • Mercer IPE: Impact, Communication, Innovation, Knowledge, Risk
    • Evaluates the job, not the jobholder

    Definition. Job evaluation is a systematic process for determining the relative worth of jobs within an organisation, so that jobs can be placed in a hierarchy and grouped into grades.

    Why it is needed. Market data alone cannot produce a coherent structure. Many jobs have no reliable market match, survey data fluctuates, and pricing each job separately from the market produces anomalies such as a job being paid more than the job that supervises it. Job evaluation provides a stable internal order to which market data is later attached.

    Two things job evaluation does not do. It does not assess the performance of the jobholder, and it does not set salaries directly. It produces an order of jobs and, in analytical methods, a measure of the distance between them.

    Methods

    Method Type How it works Strengths Weaknesses Typical use
    RankingNon-analytical, whole-jobJobs are compared as wholes and placed in order, often by paired comparisonQuick, simpleGives order but not the size of gaps; subjective; impractical for many jobsSmall organisations (< 30 jobs)
    Classification / gradingNon-analytical, whole-jobGrade descriptions are written first; each job is slotted into the best-fitting gradeEasy to explain; quick to applyJobs often fit more than one grade; descriptions become outdatedGovernment and public-sector pay levels
    Factor comparisonAnalyticalBenchmark jobs are ranked factor by factor and money values attached to each factorDetailedComplex; hard to maintain; rarely used todayHistorical
    Point-factorAnalyticalEach job is scored on defined factors with weighted levels; total points determine job sizeConsistent, explainable, shows size differencesRequires good job data and trained evaluators; appears objective even though weights are judgementsMost large companies

    How point-factor evaluation works

    A point-factor scheme defines a small number of factors that represent what makes one job larger than another, typically knowledge, problem-solving and accountability. Each factor has several levels, each with a written definition and a number of points. Evaluators read the JD, choose the level that best fits on each factor, and add the points. The total represents the job’s size.

    Worked example (illustrative points).

    Factor Shift Engineer, cement plant Purchase Executive, corporate office
    KnowledgeEngineering degree plus 3–5 years’ plant experience: 160Graduate plus 2–3 years in procurement: 120
    Problem solvingDiagnoses breakdowns under time pressure, guided by procedures: 90Compares quotations within an approved vendor list: 60
    AccountabilityOutput of a production line during a shift; safety of about 25 people: 130Purchase orders up to a set value, approved by a manager: 80
    Total380260

    The result is a reasoned statement that the shift engineer’s job is larger, and by roughly how much, which can be applied consistently to every job in the organisation.

    Proprietary systems used in India

    Organisations usually license an established system rather than design their own, because it is tested and allows comparison with other companies using the same system.

    System Factors Output Notes
    Korn Ferry Hay Guide Chart-ProfileKnow-how (technical depth, breadth of management, human-relations skill), Problem solving (thinking environment and thinking challenge, expressed as a percentage of know-how), Accountability (freedom to act, magnitude, impact on results); working conditions for some rolesHay points and Hay gradeThe longest-established method; widely used globally
    Mercer International Position Evaluation (IPE)Impact, Communication, Innovation, Knowledge, Risk (optional)Position classIntegrates directly with Mercer salary surveys; common in Indian conglomerates and MNC subsidiaries
    WTW Global Grading SystemFunctional knowledge, business expertise, leadership, problem solving, nature of impact, area of impact, interpersonal skillsGlobal gradeIntegrates with WTW surveys

    Limitations to be able to discuss. The choice of factors and weights is a value judgement. A scheme that weights budget size heavily and emotional or physical demands lightly will rate some jobs, such as care or customer-facing roles, lower. For this reason, pay-equity reviews examine the evaluation scheme as well as individual salaries. Evaluation also needs governance: an evaluation committee, documented rationales, and periodic re-evaluation when jobs change.

    2.4Grades

    In short

    A grade groups jobs of similar size so they share one pay range. Grades turn many job evaluation scores into a manageable structure.

    Recall

    • Grade = band of job-evaluation points
    • Grade ≠ title ≠ level
    • Narrow grades vs broadbanding
    • Typical number: 10–15 for a mid-size company

    Definition. A grade is a group of jobs of broadly similar size, defined by a band of job-evaluation scores, that share the same pay range and often the same benefits eligibility.

    How grades are formed. Jobs are listed in order of evaluation scores and grouped where natural breaks occur, or into bands of equal width. For example, all jobs scoring between 340 and 400 points might form grade M1; the shift engineer (380) falls in M1 and the purchase executive (260) two grades lower.

    Grades, titles and levels are different things. A title describes the work (“Shift Engineer”). A level describes progression within a job family (associate, senior, lead). A grade describes job size for pay purposes. Organisations align the three in a job architecture so that each can be read from the others (Chapter 6).

    Number and width of grades.

    Design Characteristics Advantages Disadvantages Suited to
    Many narrow grades (15–25)Small differences between gradesFrequent promotions; tight cost controlBureaucratic; every job change triggers a regrading requestHierarchical, large or public organisations
    Fewer, wider grades (8–12)ModerateBalance of control and flexibilityRequires good range managementMost corporates
    Broadbanding (4–6 bands)Very wide bands, often 100%+ spreadEncourages lateral moves and skill growth; fits flat organisationsWeak cost control; promotions less visible; managers have more discretionFlat, project-based or knowledge organisations

    2.5Salary benchmarking and pay positioning

    In short

    Benchmarking compares the organisation’s pay with the market for matched jobs. Positioning is the decision about where to stand relative to that market.

    Recall

    • Survey houses: Aon, Mercer, WTW, Korn Ferry
    • Match on content, not title
    • P25, P50 (median), P75
    • Lead, match, lag; segmented strategy
    • Age the data to a common date

    Definition. Salary benchmarking is the comparison of an organisation’s pay for specific jobs with pay for comparable jobs in a defined external market.

    Data sources

    Source How the data is produced Reliability Best use
    Salary surveys (Aon, Mercer, WTW, Korn Ferry)Participating companies submit employee-level pay data matched to the survey’s job catalogue; results are aggregated and anonymisedHigh: verified, job-matched, consistent definitionsSetting structures and midpoints
    Custom / club surveysA group of peer companies share data directly, through a consultantHigh for the peer groupIndustry-specific roles
    Job portals and review sites (Naukri, LinkedIn, Glassdoor, AmbitionBox)Job postings and self-reported salariesLow to moderate: unverified, inconsistent job definitionsSpotting trends and emerging skills
    Recruitment intelligenceCandidates’ current pay and expectations, offer declinesModerate: current but anecdotalSignalling when a range has fallen behind

    Three rules for using survey data correctly

    1. Match jobs on content, not title. A job is matched to a survey position when its core responsibilities substantially correspond to the survey’s description. Titles vary too much between companies to be reliable. This is why accurate JDs (Section 2.2) are a precondition for benchmarking.

    2. Read the distribution, not one number. Surveys report percentiles. The 25th percentile (P25) is the pay level below which a quarter of the market falls; the median (P50) divides the market in half; the 75th percentile (P75) is exceeded by only a quarter of the market.

    3. Use a common date and the right market. Survey data is collected at a fixed date, so it is “aged” forward to the date of use by the expected market movement. The comparison market should be the employers you actually compete with for that talent, which may differ by job family: a cement company competes with other manufacturers for plant engineers, but with all employers in the city for finance and IT staff.

    Pay positioning

    Position Typical target When it makes sense Trade-off
    LeadP65–P75Scarce skills; growth phases; roles critical to strategyHigher fixed cost
    MatchP50Most roles in most organisationsNeutral; competes on other parts of the offer
    LagBelow P50Strong non-cash offer (stability, brand, learning, location benefits)Higher attrition risk for mobile talent

    Best practice: segment. Most organisations do not apply one position to every job. A segmented strategy matches the market for most roles and leads it for a small number of scarce or critical skills. In an Indian cement company, for example, that could mean matching for most plant and corporate roles while leading for process engineers, safety specialists and digital roles, and compensating for remote locations through housing, township and education benefits.

    2.6Pay ranges

    In short

    Each grade has a pay range with a minimum, midpoint and maximum. The midpoint reflects the market at the chosen position; the width allows for growth in the role.

    Recall

    • Midpoint = market reference
    • Spread = (max − min) ÷ min
    • Min = mid ÷ (1 + spread/2)
    • Midpoint progression
    • Overlap

    Definition. A pay range is the band of pay an organisation is willing to pay for jobs in a grade, from a minimum to a maximum, around a midpoint.

    Why a range rather than a single rate. People in the same job differ in experience and proficiency. A newly promoted person is still learning the role; an experienced person contributes more. The range allows pay to progress with proficiency while keeping a ceiling linked to the size of the job.

    Point Meaning Who is typically paid here
    MinimumLowest pay for the jobNew entrants to the job, still learning it
    MidpointPay for a fully competent jobholder; set at the market level for the chosen positioningExperienced, fully proficient employees
    MaximumHighest pay the organisation will pay for the jobLong-serving, consistently strong performers; beyond this, higher pay requires a larger job

    Design parameters

    Parameter Definition Typical values Effect of getting it wrong
    Range spread(Maximum − Minimum) ÷ Minimum30–40% for junior and operational grades; 40–60% for professional and managerial; 60–80% or more for executiveToo narrow: experienced people hit the maximum quickly. Too wide: weak cost control
    Midpoint progressionPercentage difference between midpoints of adjacent grades8–15% at lower grades; 15–25% at senior gradesToo small: promotions carry little reward
    OverlapExtent to which adjacent ranges overlapModerate overlap is normalToo much: grades lose meaning; experienced staff in a lower grade out-earn new managers above them

    Worked example. A grade’s market median is ₹8.4 lakh, the organisation matches the median, and the chosen spread is 50%.

    Formula Result
    MidpointMarket P50₹8.40 lakh
    MinimumMidpoint ÷ (1 + spread ÷ 2) = 8.4 ÷ 1.25₹6.72 lakh
    MaximumMinimum × (1 + spread) = 6.72 × 1.5₹10.08 lakh

    2.7Positioning individuals in the range

    In short

    Compa-ratio and range penetration show where each person’s pay sits. They are used to find under- and over-payment and to target increments.

    Recall

    • Compa-ratio = salary ÷ midpoint × 100
    • Range penetration = (salary − min) ÷ (max − min) × 100
    • Red circle (above max), green circle (below min)
    • Pay compression

    Compa-ratio

    Definition. Compa-ratio = employee’s salary ÷ midpoint of the employee’s pay range × 100.

    Compa-ratio Usual interpretation Typical action
    Below 80Below the range minimum or very newCheck for green circle; bring to minimum
    80–90New to the role, or underpaid relative to proficiencyIf performing well, prioritise in increments
    90–110Around target for a competent jobholderNormal increments
    110–120Very experienced, or high relative to the jobSmaller fixed increases; consider bonus
    Above 120Likely above maximumCheck for red circle

    Group compa-ratio (total actual pay ÷ total midpoints for a team or function) shows whether a whole population is drifting above or below the structure.

    CorrectionThe compendium (p. 30) defines compa-ratio as salary relative to the market average. The standard definition uses the midpoint of the employee’s own range. The two coincide only when the midpoint equals the market average.

    Range penetration

    Definition. Range penetration = (salary − range minimum) ÷ (range maximum − range minimum) × 100. It expresses how far through the range a salary sits, from 0% at the minimum to 100% at the maximum. It is more intuitive than compa-ratio when ranges differ in width.

    Worked example: three employees in one grade

    Range: minimum ₹6.72 lakh, midpoint ₹8.40 lakh, maximum ₹10.08 lakh.

    Employee Situation Salary Compa-ratio Range penetration Diagnosis
    AJoined 8 months ago₹7.10 lakh8511%Normal for a new jobholder
    B4 years in role, strong performer₹7.60 lakh9026%Underpaid relative to proficiency; a new hire was recently brought in at ₹8.2 lakh
    C14 years in role₹10.50 lakh125Above 100%Red circle

    Out-of-range cases and compression

    Case Definition Standard treatment
    Green circleSalary below the range minimumRaise to the minimum, immediately or over one to two cycles
    Red circleSalary above the range maximumNo pay cut; freeze or reduce fixed increases; reward performance through one-time payments until the range moves up
    Pay compressionLittle or no pay difference between experienced staff and new hires or juniors, usually because market pay rose faster than internal incrementsTargeted market correction for affected incumbents; review entry-level ranges
    Pay inversionNew hires or subordinates paid more than experienced staff or their managersAs for compression, with priority

    Employee B in the example above illustrates compression, the most common cause of complaints about pay fairness.

    2.8Increments and the merit matrix

    In short

    The annual increment budget is distributed by a merit matrix that combines performance rating with position in the range, so good performers who are low in the range move up fastest.

    Recall

    • Increment budget (India 2026: ~9%)
    • Merit matrix: rating × compa-ratio
    • Promotion increase is separate
    • Lump sum for red circles

    Increment budget. Each year the organisation sets an overall budget for salary increases, as a percentage of the salary bill, based on market movement, inflation, business performance and ability to pay. In India, Aon’s survey reported an average actual increase of 8.9% in 2025 and a projected 9.1% for 2026, with real estate, infrastructure and non-banking financial companies above the average and technology services below it.

    The merit matrix. The budget is distributed using a matrix of performance rating and compa-ratio. Illustrative matrix for a budget of about 9%:

    Rating Compa-ratio below 90 90–110 Above 110
    Outstanding15%12%9%
    Exceeds expectations12%10%7%
    Meets expectations9%7%4%
    Below expectations0–3%0%0%

    The logic. For the same rating, a person low in the range receives a larger increase than a person high in the range. Over several years this moves strong performers towards the midpoint and slows the growth of salaries already above it. In the worked example, employees B and C with the same “exceeds” rating would receive about 10% and 7% respectively.

    Related practices.

    Practice Purpose
    Promotion increaseSeparate from the merit increase; usually brings the person at least to the new grade’s minimum, often with a fixed percentage on top
    Lump-sum merit paymentOne-time payment instead of a fixed increase for red circles or high-range performers, so fixed costs do not grow
    Market adjustmentOff-cycle correction for jobs or individuals that have fallen behind the market (e.g., compression cases)
    CalibrationManagers review ratings together before increments are applied, so the matrix is fed by consistent ratings (Chapter 3)

    2.9Interview questions on this part

    In short

    Most questions on this part test whether you can connect the steps and justify each one. Answer with the principle first, then the mechanism, then an example.

    How would you design a salary structure for an organisation that does not have one?

    Start with job analysis and standard job descriptions, because every later step depends on accurate job data. Evaluate jobs using a point-factor method such as Mercer IPE or Hay to establish internal relativities, and group them into grades. Benchmark each grade against salary surveys, matching on job content, and agree a pay positioning with leadership, usually the median for most roles and higher for scarce skills. Set range midpoints from the market data and range widths according to level. Place employees in the structure, deal with those below the minimum and above the maximum over one or two cycles, and use a merit matrix for future increments. Train managers to explain the structure.

    What is the difference between job analysis and job evaluation?

    Job analysis describes what a job involves and produces the job description and specification. Job evaluation uses that information to judge the job’s size relative to other jobs and produces a ranking or points score used for grading. Analysis is descriptive; evaluation is comparative.

    An employee with a compa-ratio of 85 and an outstanding rating, and another with a compa-ratio of 115 and the same rating: who should get the higher increase, and why?

    The first. Both performed equally well, but the first is paid well below the target for the job, while the second is already paid above it. A merit matrix gives a higher percentage to the first to move them towards the midpoint, and may give the second a smaller fixed increase plus a one-time bonus so that performance is still rewarded without pushing fixed pay further above the job’s value.

    New hires are being paid more than existing staff in the same role. What would you do?

    Confirm it with data (compa-ratios of incumbents versus recent offers) and identify the cause, usually market movement faster than internal increments. Make targeted market corrections for affected incumbents, prioritised by performance and retention risk; review the range for the role so offers fall within it; and track regretted attrition in the group. This is a compression problem and is primarily a fairness issue for the existing team.

    Sources: Adams, J. S. (1965), "Inequity in Social Exchange", Advances in Experimental Social Psychology. Korn Ferry, Mercer and WTW published method descriptions (Hay Guide Chart-Profile, IPE, Global Grading System). Aon, Salary Increase and Turnover Survey, India (February 2026). Point scores, salaries, range parameters and matrix percentages are illustrative of common practice, not survey or legal standards.

    Pay in India: CTC, Components and Statutory Pay

    Chapter 2 · Compensation and Benefits · Part 2 of 3

    What this part covers. How total pay is packaged in Indian organisations: the meaning of CTC, gross and net pay, the main salary components and their tax treatment, the definition of “wages” under the Code on Wages and its 50% rule, and the statutory elements every HR professional must be able to explain and calculate: provident fund, ESI, gratuity, statutory bonus, professional tax and income tax deduction at source.

    Why it matters for interviews. Indian panels routinely test practical payroll knowledge, especially since the Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025. Expect calculation questions (gratuity, PF, in-hand pay) and questions on how the new wage definition changes salary structures.

    2.10CTC, gross pay and net pay

    In short

    CTC is the employer’s total annual cost for an employee. Gross pay is salary before employee deductions. Net pay is what reaches the bank account. The gaps between them are statutory contributions, variable pay, insured benefits, taxes and deductions.

    Recall

    • CTC = gross + employer contributions + variable + other employer costs
    • Net = gross − employee PF − PT − TDS − other deductions
    • Variable pay in CTC is a target, not a guarantee

    Cost to Company (CTC) is the total amount an employer expects to spend on an employee in a year. It is an employer’s budgeting figure that has become the standard way Indian offers are expressed.

    Gross salary is the total of the salary components paid to the employee before any employee-side deductions: basic pay, dearness allowance, house rent allowance, special allowance and other allowances.

    Net salary (take-home or in-hand pay) is gross salary minus employee-side deductions: the employee’s provident fund contribution, ESI contribution where applicable, professional tax, income tax deducted at source (TDS), and any voluntary deductions such as loan repayments.

    The relationships can be written as:

    • CTC = Gross salary + employer’s statutory contributions (PF, ESI where applicable, gratuity provision) + target variable pay + other employer-paid costs included in the offer (insurance premiums, meal cards, car lease, etc.).
    • Net pay = Gross salary − employee PF − employee ESI (if applicable) − professional tax − TDS − other deductions.

    Illustrative CTC structure

    The structure below shows a typical offer of about ₹18 lakh for a management graduate. It is illustrative; actual structures vary by company and are covered by Sections 2.11 and 2.12.

    Component ₹ per year Explanation
    Basic7,50,000Set at about 50% of fixed pay so that excluded allowances do not exceed 50% of total remuneration (Section 2.12)
    House rent allowance (HRA)3,75,000Typically 40–50% of basic; partly tax-exempt only under the old tax regime
    Special allowance3,75,000Balancing figure; fully taxable
    Gross salary15,00,000
    Employer PF36,00012% of PF wages capped at ₹25,000 a month (₹3,000 × 12); some employers contribute 12% of the full basic
    Gratuity provision36,0754.81% of basic; paid only once the employee becomes eligible (Section 2.15)
    Group medical and term-life premiums15,000Included in CTC by some employers, shown separately by others
    Target performance bonus1,80,000About 10% of CTC; actual payout depends on individual and company performance
    CTC18,00,000 (approx.)

    Approximate monthly take-home for this structure under the new tax regime: gross ₹15,00,000, less the ₹75,000 standard deduction, gives taxable income of ₹14,25,000 and tax of about ₹97,500 including cess. Deducting employee PF of ₹36,000 and tax leaves about ₹13.66 lakh a year, or roughly ₹1.14 lakh a month, before any bonus. The CTC figure of ₹18 lakh therefore corresponds to about ₹1.14 lakh a month in hand.

    Why CTC can mislead, and how to compare offers

    CTC combines guaranteed pay, contingent pay and employer costs the employee never receives in cash. Two offers with the same CTC can differ in take-home pay by ₹1.5–2 lakh a year, depending on:

    • the share of variable pay and how reliably it pays out;
    • whether PF is calculated on the full basic or on the statutory ceiling;
    • whether insurance premiums and gratuity are counted inside the CTC;
    • one-time items such as joining bonuses that inflate first-year CTC.

    The correct comparison is guaranteed annual cash + realistic expected variable + value of benefits, followed by take-home pay after tax.

    Use caseCampus offers are often compared on CTC alone. When advising candidates or designing campus offers, show fixed pay, target variable pay and expected monthly take-home separately. It reduces disappointment at the first payslip and the offer drop-outs that follow.

    2.11Salary components and their tax treatment

    In short

    Indian salaries are split into components because tax law, labour law and benefits are each calculated on different components. Since the new tax regime became the default, tax-driven structuring matters less, and the wage-code definition of wages matters more.

    Recall

    • Basic, DA, HRA, special allowance, LTA, reimbursements
    • New regime is the default
    • HRA and LTA exemptions only in the old regime
    • Income-tax Act, 2025 in force from 1 April 2026

    Why salaries have components. Different laws and benefits use different bases. PF, gratuity and bonus are calculated on “wages” (mainly basic and DA). Income tax treats some allowances differently from others under the old regime. Leave encashment and some benefits are linked to basic pay. Splitting pay into components lets an employer manage each of these.

    Dearness allowance (DA)Compensation for inflation; common in government, PSUs and unionised manufacturing, linked to the consumer price indexFully taxableFully taxable
    House rent allowance (HRA)Allowance for rented accommodationExempt up to the lowest of: actual HRA; rent paid minus 10% of salary; 50% of salary in Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai or 40% elsewhere (salary = basic + DA)Fully taxable

    Income tax: what HR needs to know

    • The new tax regime is the default for individuals; employees can opt for the old regime if they prefer its exemptions and deductions.
    • New regime slabs: nil up to ₹4 lakh; 5% for ₹4–8 lakh; 10% for ₹8–12 lakh; 15% for ₹12–16 lakh; 20% for ₹16–20 lakh; 25% for ₹20–24 lakh; 30% above ₹24 lakh. A rebate makes taxable income up to ₹12 lakh tax-free, and salaried employees also get a ₹75,000 standard deduction, so salary up to about ₹12.75 lakh attracts no tax. A 4% health and education cess applies to the tax.
    • The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961 from 1 April 2026. Section numbers changed (for example, the familiar “Section 80C” and “Section 87A” now sit under new numbers); rates and slabs carried over. In interviews, refer to provisions by name rather than old section numbers.
    • Consequence for pay design: because most employees now choose the new regime, flexible benefit plans built around HRA, LTA and meal-voucher exemptions have lost much of their tax value. Employers are shifting from tax-driven structures towards simpler ones, with employer NPS contributions remaining one of the few tax-efficient tools under the new regime.

    2.12“Wages” under the Code on Wages: the 50% rule

    In short

    The Code on Wages defines wages as basic pay, DA and retaining allowance. If excluded allowances exceed 50% of total remuneration, the excess is added back to wages. Wages drive PF, gratuity, bonus and overtime, so low-basic structures now cost more.

    Recall

    • Wages = basic + DA + retaining allowance
    • Excluded items > 50% → excess added back
    • Effects: higher gratuity, possibly higher PF, lower take-home
    • In force from 21 November 2025

    Before the Codes. Each labour law had its own definition of wages. Many employers kept basic pay at 25–40% of gross and paid the rest as allowances, which reduced PF contributions, gratuity and bonus, all calculated on basic and DA.

    The uniform definition. The Code on Wages, 2019, in force with the other Labour Codes from 21 November 2025, defines wages as all remuneration expressed in money, including basic pay, dearness allowance and retaining allowance, and then lists exclusions such as HRA, conveyance allowance, statutory bonus, overtime allowance, commission, employer contributions to PF and pension, and gratuity.

    The 50% rule. If the excluded items together exceed 50% of total remuneration, the amount by which they exceed 50% is added back to wages. The purpose is to stop employers from artificially splitting pay into allowances to reduce statutory dues.

    Worked example. Monthly remuneration ₹1,00,000: basic ₹30,000, HRA ₹25,000, special allowance ₹45,000.

    Step Calculation Result
    Excluded itemsHRA + special allowance = 25,000 + 45,000₹70,000 (70% of total)
    Permitted exclusion50% of ₹1,00,000₹50,000
    Excess added back70,000 − 50,000₹20,000
    Wages for statutory purposes30,000 + 20,000₹50,000

    PF (where calculated on actual wages), gratuity and bonus are then computed on ₹50,000 rather than ₹30,000. The Labour Ministry’s FAQs (March 2026) clarified that overtime counts among the excluded items for this test.

    Effects on organisations.

    Effect Explanation
    Higher gratuity liabilityGratuity is calculated on wages; a higher wage base raises both the provision and the eventual payout
    Possibly higher PFWhere PF is paid on actual wages rather than the statutory ceiling
    Lower take-home payIf CTC stays constant and more of it goes to PF and gratuity
    CTC restructuringMany organisations raised basic + DA to about 50% of total remuneration in 2025–26
    Leave encashment and retrenchment compensationCalculated on the wider wage base where linked to wages

    Precision pointThe Code does not say that “basic must be 50% of CTC”. It caps exclusions at 50% of total remuneration and adds back any excess. Organisations often set basic + DA at around 50% because it is the simplest way to comply.

    2.13Provident fund (EPF, EPS, EDLI)

    In short

    The EPF scheme provides retirement savings, a pension and life cover. Employer and employee each contribute 12% of PF wages. From 17 September 2026, coverage is mandatory for PF wages up to ₹25,000 a month.

    Recall

    • Applies to establishments with 20+ employees
    • Wage ceiling ₹25,000 (was ₹15,000)
    • Employer 12% = 8.33% EPS + 3.67% EPF
    • Max EPS ₹2,083/month
    • EDLI 0.5%; admin 0.5%

    What it is. The Employees’ Provident Fund scheme, administered by the Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO) and now governed by the Code on Social Security, 2020, has three parts:

    Scheme Purpose Funded by
    EPF (Employees’ Provident Fund)Retirement savings account with annual interest (8.25% for 2024–25)Employee 12% + part of the employer’s 12%
    EPS (Employees’ Pension Scheme)Monthly pension after retirement (age 58), and for family on deathEmployer’s 8.33% share, up to the ceiling; government contribution of 1.16% on wages up to ₹15,000
    EDLI (Employees’ Deposit Linked Insurance)Life cover for the family if the member dies in serviceEmployer 0.5%

    Coverage. The scheme applies to establishments employing 20 or more persons. Membership is mandatory for employees whose PF wages are within the wage ceiling, which was raised from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 a month with effect from 17 September 2026 (notification S.O. 5109(E)), the first increase since 2014. Employees above the ceiling can be covered voluntarily.

    Contributions (on PF wages, i.e., basic + DA plus any wage-code add-back).

    Contributor Rate Notes
    Employee12%Deducted from salary; can contribute more voluntarily (VPF)
    Employer: EPS8.33%On wages up to ₹25,000, so at most ₹2,083 a month
    Employer: EPF3.67% (or the balance of 12%)Balance of the employer’s 12% after EPS
    Employer: EDLI0.5%Insurance
    Employer: administration charges0.5%

    Worked example. PF wages ₹25,000 a month. Employee contribution ₹3,000. Employer contribution ₹3,000, of which ₹2,083 goes to EPS and ₹917 to EPF, plus ₹125 for EDLI and ₹125 administration charges.

    A common design choice. For employees earning above the ceiling, employers choose between contributing 12% of the ceiling (₹3,000 a month) or 12% of actual PF wages. The first keeps take-home pay higher and costs less; the second builds a larger retirement corpus. Many organisations offer employees the choice where the scheme rules allow it.

    Effect of the ceiling increase. Employees with PF wages between ₹15,000 and ₹25,000 who were previously outside mandatory coverage, or contributing on ₹15,000, now contribute on their actual wages up to ₹25,000. For PF wages of ₹20,000, the monthly employee deduction rises from ₹1,800 to ₹2,400. The employer’s own contribution cannot be recovered from the employee’s salary.

    2.14Employees’ State Insurance (ESI)

    In short

    ESI is a contributory health and social-insurance scheme for lower-paid employees, run by ESIC. It covers medical care and cash benefits for sickness, maternity, disablement and death.

    Recall

    • 10+ employees
    • Gross ≤ ₹21,000/month (₹25,000 for persons with disability)
    • Employee 0.75%, employer 3.25%
    • Calculated on gross, not PF wages

    What it is. The Employees’ State Insurance scheme, administered by the Employees’ State Insurance Corporation (ESIC), provides medical care and cash benefits to covered employees and their families.

    Coverage. Establishments with 10 or more employees in notified areas. Employees with gross wages of up to ₹21,000 a month (₹25,000 for persons with disabilities) are covered.

    Contributions. Employee 0.75% and employer 3.25% of gross wages. Unlike PF, ESI is calculated on gross wages, so payroll must keep the two bases separate.

    Benefit What it provides
    MedicalFull medical care for the employee and family through ESI hospitals and dispensaries
    SicknessCash benefit of 70% of wages for certified sickness, up to 91 days a year
    MaternityCash benefit of full wages during maternity leave (in place of the employer paying maternity benefit)
    DisablementCash benefit for temporary or permanent disablement from employment injury
    Dependants’ benefitMonthly payments to dependants on death from employment injury
    Unemployment allowanceLimited cash support for insured persons who lose employment, under specified conditions

    When ESI does not apply, employers remain liable under the employee compensation provisions for workplace injury and must fund maternity benefit themselves; this is one reason group medical and personal-accident insurance is standard for employees above the ESI ceiling.

    2.15Gratuity

    In short

    Gratuity is a statutory lump sum paid on leaving after qualifying service: 15 days’ wages for every completed year. Fixed-term employees qualify after one year.

    Recall

    • 15 × last wages × years ÷ 26
    • 5 years (waived on death or disablement)
    • Fixed-term: 1 year, pro rata
    • Ceiling ₹20 lakh
    • Pay within 30 days
    • Provision ≈ 4.81% of basic

    What it is. Gratuity is a terminal benefit that recognises long service. It is not linked to performance. It was governed by the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, and is now part of the Code on Social Security, 2020.

    Applicability and eligibility.

    Condition Rule
    Establishments coveredThose employing 10 or more persons
    Events on which payableSuperannuation, retirement, resignation, death, disablement due to accident or disease, termination; also expiry of a fixed-term contract
    Qualifying service5 years of continuous service; not required on death or disablement
    Fixed-term employeesEligible after 1 year of service, on a pro-rata basis (Social Security Code)
    RoundingService of more than 6 months in the final year counts as a full year
    Ceiling₹20 lakh (government may revise)
    Payment deadlineWithin 30 days of becoming payable; interest is due on delay
    ForfeiturePossible, wholly or partly, for termination due to specified misconduct (e.g., wilful damage to employer property, riotous conduct, an offence involving moral turpitude)

    Formula (for employees paid monthly): Gratuity = 15 × last drawn wages × completed years of service ÷ 26. Here “wages” means basic + DA (now including any wage-code add-back), 15 represents fifteen days’ wages per year, and 26 the working days in a month.

    Worked example. Last drawn basic + DA ₹60,000 a month; service of 7 years and 8 months, which rounds to 8 years. Gratuity = 15 × 60,000 × 8 ÷ 26 = ₹2,76,923.

    Funding. Employers either set aside a provision each year (about 4.81% of basic, since 15/26 ÷ 12 ≈ 4.81%) based on an actuarial valuation, or buy a group gratuity policy from an insurer such as LIC. The wider wage definition (Section 2.12) and fixed-term eligibility both increased gratuity liabilities after November 2025.

    2.16Statutory bonus

    In short

    The statutory bonus is a minimum profit-related payment to lower-paid employees, between 8.33% and 20% of wages, now governed by the Code on Wages.

    Recall

    • 20+ employees
    • Wages ≤ ₹21,000/month; 30 days worked
    • Min 8.33%, max 20%
    • Calculated on ₹7,000 or minimum wage, whichever higher
    • Paid within 8 months of year-end

    What it is. The statutory bonus, previously under the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965, is now part of the Code on Wages. It is distinct from the performance bonuses employers pay voluntarily (Part 3).

    Element Rule
    Establishments20 or more employees
    Eligible employeesWages up to ₹21,000 a month and at least 30 working days in the year
    Minimum bonus8.33% of wages (payable even without profit), subject to rules for new establishments
    Maximum bonus20% of wages, depending on the “allocable surplus” from profits
    Calculation baseWages of ₹7,000 a month or the applicable minimum wage, whichever is higher
    Set-on and set-offExcess or shortfall of allocable surplus is carried forward to later years
    TimingWithin 8 months of the close of the accounting year
    Contract labourUnder the central rules (May 2026), if a contractor fails to pay the minimum bonus, the principal employer must pay it

    Example. An eligible employee with a minimum wage of ₹12,000 a month: bonus base is ₹12,000 (higher than ₹7,000). Minimum annual bonus = 12,000 × 12 × 8.33% = ₹11,995; maximum = 12,000 × 12 × 20% = ₹28,800.

    2.18Interview questions on this part

    In short

    Calculation questions are common. State the formula, apply it to a number, then mention one practical implication.

    What is the difference between CTC and take-home pay?

    CTC is the employer’s total annual cost, including employer PF, gratuity provision, target variable pay and insured benefits. Take-home pay is gross salary minus the employee’s PF, professional tax, income tax and other deductions. For a ₹18 lakh CTC with ₹15 lakh gross, take-home under the new tax regime is roughly ₹1.1 lakh a month.

    How does the Code on Wages affect salary structures?

    It defines wages as basic, DA and retaining allowance, and adds back any excluded allowances above 50% of total remuneration. Because PF, gratuity and bonus are calculated on wages, organisations with low basic pay face higher gratuity and possibly higher PF costs, and employees may see slightly lower take-home pay. Most organisations responded by restructuring so that basic and DA are around half of total remuneration, and by communicating the change before it reached payslips.

    Calculate gratuity for an employee with 9 years and 7 months of service and last drawn basic + DA of ₹45,000.

    Service rounds up to 10 years because the final part-year exceeds six months. Gratuity = 15 × 45,000 × 10 ÷ 26 = ₹2,59,615. It is payable within 30 days of leaving.

    What changed with the EPF wage ceiling in 2026?

    The ceiling rose from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 a month from 17 September 2026. Coverage is now mandatory for PF wages up to ₹25,000, the employer’s EPS contribution is calculated on wages up to the new ceiling (a maximum of ₹2,083 a month), and employees in the ₹15,000–25,000 band contribute more, which lowers take-home pay but increases retirement savings.

    Sources: Code on Wages, 2019 (definition of wages) and Ministry of Labour and Employment FAQs (March 2026); Code on Social Security, 2020 and Social Security (Central) Rules, 2026; KPMG Flash News on the final central rules (10 May 2026); EPFO notification S.O. 5109(E), 17 September 2026; Income-tax Act, 2025 and Finance Act provisions on new-regime slabs. The CTC structure, take-home estimate and example figures are illustrative.

    What this part covers.     2.19Compensation philosophy and pay strategy

    In short

    A compensation philosophy states how an organisation intends to use pay to support its strategy. It answers a fixed set of design questions, which the compendium groups as the “6Ps”.

    Recall

    • Positioning (lead, match, lag)
    • Internal equity
    • Pay mix (fixed, STI, LTI, benefits)
    • Pay for performance (differentiation)
    • Ranges and structure
    • Transparency and communication

    Definition. A compensation philosophy is a short statement, approved by leadership, that sets the principles for pay decisions: what the organisation rewards, how it compares with the market, how much pay varies with performance, and how openly pay is communicated. It guides structure design, offers, increments and incentive plans, so that individual decisions are consistent.

    The design questions a philosophy answers

    Decision Question Options and examples
    Market positioningWhere do we pay relative to the market?Lead (P65–P75), match (P50), lag (below P50); often segmented by skill (Section 2.5)
    Internal equityHow do we compare jobs internally?Job evaluation and grades (Sections 2.3–2.4)
    Pay mixWhat share is fixed, short-term variable, long-term variable and benefits?Fixed-heavy (stability), variable-heavy (results focus), balanced
    Pay for performanceHow much more do top performers receive?Size of increment and bonus differentials between ratings
    StructureHow wide are ranges, how many grades?Narrow grades, standard ranges, broadbands
    TransparencyWhat do employees know about pay?Nothing beyond own pay; ranges by grade; full transparency

    Pay mix varies with level. The share of pay at risk usually rises with seniority, because senior roles have more influence over results.

    Level Typical mix in Indian corporates (fixed : short-term variable : long-term)
    Operator / workman (unionised)Almost entirely fixed; production or attendance incentives set in wage settlements
    Officer and junior manager85–95 : 5–15 : 0
    Middle manager80–85 : 10–20 : 0–5
    Senior leadership (listed company)50–65 : 15–25 : 20–35
    Sales roles50–70 : 30–50 : 0

    These ranges are indicative of common practice; actual mixes vary widely by sector.

    Examples of distinct philosophies

    Organisation Philosophy Design consequences
    NetflixPay top of personal market; no pay for adequate performanceHigh fixed salary, little or no bonus, employees can choose the cash/stock split
    Google (Alphabet)Reward impact and ownershipCompetitive base, performance bonus, large RSU grants, extensive benefits
    Goldman SachsPay strongly for resultsModerate base, large discretionary bonuses
    Many Tata group companiesMarket-matching pay, stability, values and brandStructured grades, moderate variable pay, strong benefits and long tenure
    High-growth Indian start-upsConserve cash, share upsideBelow-market cash for some roles, significant ESOPs

    CorrectionThe compendium’s “6Ps” (positioning, parity, approach, mix, range, differential) is a teaching device, not a standard model, and “Pay Poisoning” on p. 28 is a typo for pay positioning. In interviews, describe these as the main design decisions of a compensation philosophy.

    2.20Short-term incentives and variable pay

    In short

    Short-term incentives reward results over a year or less. A well-designed plan has clear eligibility, a target payout, a few measurable metrics, a payout curve with threshold and cap, and a funding mechanism.

    Recall

    • Bonus, commission, gainsharing, profit sharing
    • Target %, metrics, weights
    • Threshold, target, maximum
    • Pool funding × individual modifier
    • Line of sight (Vroom)

    Definition. Short-term incentives (STIs) are variable payments linked to performance over a period of one year or less. They are re-earned each period and do not add to fixed salary.

    Types

    Type How it works Best suited to
    Annual performance bonusTarget percentage of salary, adjusted for company, unit and individual performanceMost managerial and professional roles
    Sales incentive / commissionPayment per unit of sales, revenue or margin, often with accelerators above targetSales roles with measurable individual output
    Productivity-linked incentive / gainsharingTeam or plant receives a share of measured gains in output, quality or costManufacturing plants; often negotiated in wage settlements
    Profit sharingA share of company profit distributed to employeesBroad alignment with company results; weak individual link
    Spot and project awardsOne-time recognition paymentsRewarding specific contributions quickly

    Design elements of an annual bonus plan

    Element Description Typical choices
    EligibilityWho participatesGrades above a level; minimum service in the year
    Target incentivePayout at 100% achievement, as % of salary8–15% for managers, 20–40% for senior leaders
    Metrics and weightsWhat performance is measuredCompany financial metrics (revenue, EBITDA) plus unit and individual goals; usually 3–5 metrics
    Payout curveHow payout varies with achievementThreshold (e.g., 80% of target pays 50%), target (100% pays 100%), maximum (e.g., 120% pays 150–200%)
    FundingHow the total pool is determinedPool funded by company results, then allocated by individual rating
    Individual modifierHow individual performance adjusts payoutMultiplier by rating, e.g., 0–1.5×
    GovernanceWho approves, clawback, timingRemuneration committee approval; clawback for misconduct

    Worked example. A manager with fixed pay of ₹20 lakh has a target bonus of 12% (₹2.4 lakh). Company performance funds the pool at 90%; the manager’s rating carries a 1.2× multiplier. Payout = 2.4 × 0.9 × 1.2 = ₹2.59 lakh.

    When variable pay motivates, and when it does not

    Variable pay changes behaviour when the conditions of Vroom’s expectancy theory hold. Expectancy: the employee believes effort can achieve the target. Instrumentality: payout reliably follows achievement. Valence: the amount matters to the employee, usually at least 10–15% of pay. It fails when employees cannot influence the metric, when targets are changed mid-year, or when payouts become routine and are treated as part of fixed pay.

    India contextIn large Indian IT services firms, quarterly variable pay for junior employees is linked largely to company and unit performance, such as utilisation and margins, and payouts have been reduced or deferred in weak quarters. This is a frequent discussion point: for a junior engineer with little influence over company results, such variable pay behaves more like profit sharing than a performance incentive. In 2024 TCS also linked variable pay to office attendance, using variable pay as a compliance lever.

    2.21Long-term incentives (LTIs)

    In short

    LTIs reward performance and retention over several years, usually through equity. ESOPs give the right to buy shares at a fixed price; RSUs give shares outright on vesting; performance shares vest only if targets are met.

    Recall

    • ESOP, RSU, PSU, SAR, phantom stock
    • Grant, vesting, cliff, exercise
    • ESOP taxed at exercise, then capital gains
    • SEBI (SBEB & SE) Regulations, 2021 for listed companies

    Purpose. LTIs align employees with long-term value and encourage retention, because unvested awards are forfeited on leaving. They are standard for senior roles in listed companies and are widely used at all levels in start-ups.

    Instruments

    Instrument What the employee receives Value to employee Typical users
    ESOP (employee stock option)Right to buy shares at a fixed exercise price after vestingMarket price − exercise price; zero if the price falls below exercise priceStart-ups; Indian listed companies
    RSU (restricted stock unit)Shares delivered free (or at face value) when they vestFull share value; always some valueListed MNCs, large tech companies
    Performance share unit (PSU)RSUs that vest only if performance conditions are met (e.g., relative shareholder return, EPS growth)As RSU, if conditions metSenior executives
    Stock appreciation right (SAR)Cash or shares equal to the increase in share pricePrice increase onlyCompanies wanting to limit dilution
    Phantom stockCash equal to the value or growth of notional sharesAs SAR or RSU, in cashUnlisted or family-owned companies

    Key terms. The grant is the award made today. Vesting is when the employee gains the right to the award, typically over 3–4 years. A cliff is a minimum period before any vesting, often one year. Exercise is buying the shares at the exercise price (for options).

    Worked example. An employee receives 4,000 ESOPs at an exercise price of ₹100, vesting 25% a year after a one-year cliff. After four years the share is valued at ₹400. Gain before tax = 4,000 × (400 − 100) = ₹12 lakh. If the share were ₹90, the options would be worthless; RSUs in the same situation would still be worth ₹90 each.

    Tax in India. For ESOPs, the difference between fair market value and exercise price is taxed as a perquisite (salary income) at exercise, and any further gain is taxed as capital gains on sale. Employees of eligible start-ups can defer the perquisite tax. RSUs are taxed as a perquisite on vesting.

    Regulation. Share-based schemes of listed Indian companies are governed by the SEBI (Share Based Employee Benefits and Sweat Equity) Regulations, 2021, which require shareholder approval and disclosure. Unlisted start-ups have used ESOP buybacks (Flipkart, Swiggy, Zepto, Meesho and others) to let employees realise value before listing.

    One-time and retention payments

    Payment Purpose Usual safeguard
    Joining bonusOffset forfeited bonus or equity at the previous employerRepayable if the employee leaves within 12–24 months
    Retention bonusKeep critical people through a merger, project or transitionPaid in instalments, conditional on staying
    Notice-period buyoutRelease a candidate earlier from a long notice periodLinked to a confirmed joining date

    2.22Benefits

    In short

    Benefits provide security and well-being rather than cash. In India they combine statutory benefits with voluntary insurance, retirement and leave benefits. Their value depends on employees’ perception of them as much as on their cost.

    Recall

    • Statutory: PF, ESI, gratuity, maternity, employee compensation, bonus
    • Voluntary: GMC, GTL, GPA, NPS, leave, parental benefits
    • Flexible benefits
    • Shop-floor benefits: canteen, transport, housing

    Definition. Benefits are non-cash or deferred forms of reward that protect employees against risks (illness, death, old age) and support their well-being and life outside work.

    StatutoryPF, EPS and EDLI; ESI; gratuity; maternity benefit (26 weeks); crèche for 50+ employees; employee compensation for workplace injury; statutory bonus; leave under state Shops & Establishments ActsCompliance requirement (Part 2; Chapter 11)

    Flexible benefits. A flexible plan gives employees a budget to choose from a menu (for example, higher health cover for parents, more NPS, or wellness spending). It increases perceived value at the same cost. Since most employees moved to the new tax regime, the purpose of flexible plans has shifted from tax saving to choice.

    Cost versus perceived value. Employees often underestimate the cost of benefits. Total reward statements, which show each employee the full value of pay and benefits, address this gap.

    2.23Total rewards

    In short

    Total rewards is everything employees value in the employment deal, cash and non-cash. WorldatWork’s model has five elements.

    Recall

    • Benefits
    • Well-being
    • Recognition
    • Development

    Definition. A total rewards approach treats pay, benefits, well-being, recognition and development as one integrated offer, designed to attract, motivate and retain the workforce the strategy needs.

    Element (WorldatWork) Includes Example

    Well-beingPhysical, mental, financial and social health; flexibilityEAP counselling, hybrid work, financial-literacy programmes

    Use cases. Total rewards thinking is most useful when cash is constrained or not the deciding factor. ITC, for example, attracts campus talent with cross-business exposure (FMCG, hotels, agri-business, paperboards), structured careers and stability alongside competitive pay. A remote cement plant competes for engineers through housing, schools and safety culture as much as salary. In interviews, use the framework to show that reward is broader than pay.

    2.24Pay equity, transparency and governance

    In short

    Pay equity means similar work receives similar pay regardless of gender or other protected characteristics. It is tested by separating the raw pay gap from the adjusted gap. Transparency and governance make pay decisions accountable.

    Recall

    • Raw vs adjusted gender pay gap
    • Regression for adjusted gap
    • Code on Wages: equal remuneration
    • SEBI BRSR disclosures
    • EU Pay Transparency Directive (2026)
    • NRC; Companies Act limits

    Measuring a gender pay gap

    Measure Calculation What it shows
    Raw (unadjusted) gap(Median male pay − median female pay) ÷ median male payOverall difference, driven mostly by representation: fewer women in senior grades
    Adjusted gapPay difference between men and women after controlling for grade, role, experience, performance and location, usually through regression analysisUnexplained difference that may reflect bias in pay decisions

    Method. Regress the logarithm of salary on grade, job family, experience, performance rating and location, plus a gender variable. The coefficient on gender estimates the adjusted gap. A significant adjusted gap needs correction through a dedicated budget; a large raw gap with a small adjusted gap points to problems in hiring and promotion rather than pay setting.

    Where unexplained gaps usually come from. Starting salaries anchored on previous pay, which carries forward past inequities; differences in negotiation; slower increases after career breaks and maternity leave; and job evaluation schemes that undervalue some types of work.

    Law, disclosure and transparency

    • Code on Wages: prohibits discrimination on grounds of gender, including transgender identity, in wages and recruitment for the same or similar work.
    • SEBI’s Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR): the largest listed companies disclose workforce diversity and gross wages paid to women as a share of total wages.
    • Companies Act, 2013: listed companies disclose the ratio of each director’s remuneration to the median employee’s remuneration.
    • EU Pay Transparency Directive: member states were required to implement it by June 2026; it requires pay ranges in job advertisements and gender pay-gap reporting. It affects Indian multinationals with EU operations and is shaping global practice.
    • Transparency options: disclose nothing beyond an employee’s own pay; publish ranges by grade; or full individual transparency (rare). Publishing ranges is the common middle path: it improves trust and reduces negotiation-driven gaps without disclosing individual salaries.

    Governance of executive pay in India

    • The Nomination and Remuneration Committee (NRC) of the board, required for listed and certain large companies under Section 178 of the Companies Act, recommends remuneration policy for directors and senior management.
    • Total managerial remuneration in a public company is limited to 11% of net profits, with approval required to exceed it.
    • Clawback and malus provisions, allowing recovery or cancellation of incentive pay for misconduct or restated results, are increasingly common in variable pay and LTI plans.

    2.25Interview questions on this part

    In short

    Opinion questions need a position, the reasoning, the strongest counter-argument and a practical design answer.

    Is variable pay a good motivator?

    It is when three conditions hold: employees can influence the metric, payout reliably follows achievement, and the amount is meaningful, roughly 10–15% of pay or more. These are the conditions of expectancy theory. It fails when it is linked to results employees cannot influence, when targets change mid-year, or when payouts become routine. For junior roles in large organisations, company-linked variable pay behaves more like profit sharing; for sales and senior roles, well-designed variable pay is effective.

    ESOPs or RSUs: which would you recommend?

    It depends on the company’s stage. A start-up conserving cash and offering high growth potential will usually use ESOPs, which cost nothing at grant and reward upside. A listed or mature company will usually prefer RSUs, which retain value even if the share price falls and are therefore a stronger retention tool. Both should vest over three to four years, often with a one-year cliff.

    Should salaries be transparent?

    Full individual transparency is rarely practical in India because of privacy expectations and the risk of conflict over individual cases. Publishing pay ranges by grade, explaining how increments and bonuses are decided, and auditing pay equity regularly captures most of the benefit: it builds trust, narrows negotiation-driven gaps, and prepares organisations for disclosure requirements such as the EU directive.

    A pay-equity review shows women earn 18% less than men overall but only 3% less in the same grades and roles. What does that tell you, and what would you do?

    Most of the gap comes from representation: fewer women in senior grades. The 3% adjusted gap should be corrected through a dedicated budget in the next cycle. The larger priority is the pipeline: promotion rates, hiring into senior roles, and progression after maternity leave. Offer-setting practices that anchor on previous salary should also be reviewed.
