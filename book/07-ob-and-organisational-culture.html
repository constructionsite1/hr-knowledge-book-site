    Organisational Behaviour and Culture

    Chapter 7 · Organisational Behaviour and Culture

    What this chapter covers. Personality and individual differences; values and attitudes; perception, attribution and decision biases; theories of motivation; groups and teams; conflict and negotiation; power and influence; leadership; organisational culture (Schein, the Competing Values Framework, Hofstede); and psychological safety and diversity, equity, inclusion and belonging.

    Why it matters for interviews. OB theory is rarely asked on its own but supplies the reasoning behind HR answers. Naming the relevant theory briefly and applying it (“this is an equity-theory problem: people compare their pay-to-effort ratio with colleagues’”) makes answers more rigorous.

    7.1What organisational behaviour studies

    In short

    OB studies how individuals, groups and structures affect behaviour in organisations, so that behaviour can be understood, predicted and influenced.

    Recall

    • Individual, group, organisation levels
    • Evidence-based, contingency approach
    • Why HR uses OB

    Organisational behaviour (OB) is the study of how people think, feel and act in organisations, at three levels: the individual (personality, perception, motivation), the group (teams, conflict, power, leadership) and the organisation (structure, culture, change). OB draws on psychology, sociology and anthropology and emphasises contingency: what works depends on the situation. HR practices such as selection, performance management, rewards and change management are applications of OB findings.

    7.2Personality and individual differences

    In short

    The Big Five is the best-supported personality model; conscientiousness predicts job performance across most jobs. MBTI is popular but unsuitable for selection.

    Recall

    • Big Five (OCEAN)
    • Barrick & Mount (1991)
    • Dark Triad
    • MBTI limits
    • Locus of control; self-efficacy
    • Emotional intelligence
    • Johari Window

    Personality is the relatively stable pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviours that characterises a person.

    The Big Five (five-factor model)

    Trait High scorers are… Work relevance
    Openness to experienceCurious, imaginative, open to new ideasAdaptability to change, creativity, learning
    ConscientiousnessOrganised, dependable, disciplined, achievement-orientedThe most consistent predictor of job performance across occupations (Barrick and Mount, 1991)
    ExtraversionSociable, assertive, energeticPerformance in sales and management; leadership emergence
    AgreeablenessCooperative, trusting, compassionateTeamwork and customer service; very high agreeableness can make tough decisions harder
    Neuroticism (low = emotional stability)Anxious, easily stressed, moodyLow neuroticism supports performance under pressure and job satisfaction

    Each trait is a continuum, not a category. Validated Big Five-based instruments are used in selection and development alongside other methods. A related six-factor model, HEXACO, adds honesty–humility, which is relevant to integrity.

    The Dark Triad: narcissism (grandiosity, entitlement), Machiavellianism (manipulation, cynicism about others) and psychopathy (lack of empathy, impulsivity). These traits are associated with counterproductive work behaviour and toxic leadership.

    Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), based on Carl Jung’s theory, classifies people into 16 types using four dichotomies: extraversion–introversion, sensing–intuition, thinking–feeling and judging–perceiving. It is widely used for team discussions and self-awareness, but research has found weak test–retest reliability (many people receive a different type when retested) and little evidence that type predicts job performance. It should not be used for selection or promotion decisions.

    CorrectionThe compendium presents MBTI without caveats. In an interview, say you would use it for team conversations, not for hiring or promotion, and use validated instruments for selection.

    Other individual differences

    Concept Meaning HR relevance
    Type A and Type BType A: competitive, impatient, time-pressured; Type B: relaxed, patientStress and burnout risk
    Locus of control (Rotter, 1966)Internal: belief that one controls outcomes; external: belief that outcomes depend on luck or othersInternals tend to show more initiative and job satisfaction
    Self-efficacy (Bandura)Belief in one’s ability to succeed at a specific taskPredicts effort and persistence; built through mastery experiences, role models and encouragement
    Emotional intelligence (EI)The ability to perceive, understand and manage one’s own and others’ emotionsSee below

    Emotional intelligence. Daniel Goleman’s popular model (1995) describes five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills. Peter Salovey and John Mayer’s earlier ability model treats EI as a set of measurable abilities. Research supports some link between EI and performance in emotionally demanding roles such as service and leadership, but popular claims that EI matters more than intelligence are overstated. Elements such as self-awareness and empathy can be developed through feedback and coaching.

    The Johari Window (Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham, 1955) divides self-knowledge into four areas: open (known to self and others), blind (known to others, not to self), hidden (known to self, not to others) and unknown. Feedback reduces the blind area and self-disclosure reduces the hidden area, which is the logic behind 360-degree feedback and coaching.

    7.3Values and attitudes

    In short

    Values are enduring beliefs about what is important; attitudes are evaluations of specific objects such as one’s job. Both shape behaviour at work.

    Recall

    • Terminal vs instrumental values (Rokeach)
    • Job satisfaction, commitment, engagement
    • Organisational citizenship behaviour
    • Cognitive dissonance (Festinger)

    Values are enduring beliefs about what is desirable. Milton Rokeach distinguished terminal values (desired end states, such as a comfortable life or family security) from instrumental values (preferred ways of behaving, such as honesty or ambition). Value fit between employees and organisations affects attraction, satisfaction and retention (person–organisation fit, Chapter 1).

    Attitudes are evaluations of people, objects or events. Key work attitudes are job satisfaction, organisational commitment and engagement (Chapter 5).

    Organisational citizenship behaviour (OCB) is discretionary behaviour that is not part of formal job requirements but helps the organisation, such as helping colleagues, speaking well of the organisation and volunteering for extra tasks. Engaged employees show more OCB.

    Cognitive dissonance (Leon Festinger, 1957) is the discomfort people feel when their attitudes and behaviour conflict; they reduce it by changing one or the other. Involving employees in designing a change, and therefore committing them publicly to it, can shift their attitudes towards the change.

    7.4Perception, attribution and decision-making

    In short

    People interpret others’ behaviour through attributions and biases. Structured processes and evidence reduce bias in HR decisions.

    Recall

    • Kelley: consistency, distinctiveness, consensus
    • Fundamental attribution error
    • Self-serving bias
    • Perceptual biases
    • Bounded rationality; heuristics
    • Escalation of commitment; groupthink

    Perception is the process of organising and interpreting sensory information. Because people perceive selectively, the same event can be seen differently.

    Attribution theory

    Harold Kelley proposed that people decide whether behaviour is caused internally (by the person’s ability or effort) or externally (by the situation) using three cues: consistency (does the person behave this way regularly?), distinctiveness (do they behave this way only in this situation?) and consensus (do others behave the same way in the same situation?). High consistency, low distinctiveness and low consensus suggest an internal cause.

    Two systematic errors. The fundamental attribution error is the tendency to over-attribute others’ behaviour to their character and under-attribute it to the situation: a manager calls an operator “careless” when the machine guard is badly designed. The self-serving bias is the tendency to credit one’s successes to oneself and blame failures on circumstances.

    Perceptual biases in HR decisions

    Bias Description HR example
    Halo / horn effectOne positive or negative trait colours the overall judgementFluent English taken as evidence of competence
    Contrast effectJudging someone in comparison with the previous personAverage candidate looks strong after a weak one
    Similarity (affinity) biasFavouring people like oneselfHiring from one’s own college or region
    StereotypingJudging individuals by group membership“Older employees won’t adopt the new HRIS”
    Selective perceptionNoticing what fits one’s interests or expectationsOverlooking a favoured employee’s errors
    RecencyOverweighting recent eventsAnnual rating based on the last month

    Decision-making

    Herbert Simon’s concept of bounded rationality holds that people cannot consider all options and information, so they satisfice: choose the first acceptable option. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people rely on heuristics (mental shortcuts) that produce systematic biases:

    Bias Description HR example
    AnchoringRelying too heavily on the first number or piece of informationA candidate’s current salary anchors the offer
    AvailabilityJudging likelihood by how easily examples come to mindOne dramatic resignation treated as a trend
    RepresentativenessJudging by similarity to a stereotypeAssuming a confident candidate will be a good leader
    ConfirmationSeeking information that confirms existing beliefsInterviewer looks for evidence for a first impression
    OverconfidenceOverestimating the accuracy of one’s judgementsManagers trusting their ability to “read” candidates in minutes
    Escalation of commitmentContinuing a failing course of action because of past investment (sunk cost)Persisting with a failing HRIS rollout because so much has been spent
    Ostrich effectAvoiding negative informationIgnoring early signs of attrition in a key team

    Groupthink. Irving Janis described groupthink as a mode of thinking in cohesive groups in which the desire for agreement overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives. Symptoms include illusions of invulnerability and unanimity, pressure on dissenters, self-censorship and “mindguards” who shield the group from contrary information. Remedies include appointing a devil’s advocate, inviting outside experts, leaders withholding their own views until others have spoken, and separate subgroups working on the same problem.

    De-biasing in HR processes: structured interviews and scorecards, diverse panels, independent judgements before discussion, calibration, clear criteria decided in advance, and analysis of decision outcomes by group.

    7.5Motivation

    In short

    Content theories explain what motivates people; process theories explain how motivation works. HR practices apply both.

    Recall

    • Content: Maslow, ERG, Herzberg, McClelland
    • Process: Vroom, Adams, Locke & Latham, SDT
    • Reinforcement (Skinner)
    • Pink: autonomy, mastery, purpose
    • Theory-to-practice table

    Motivation is the set of processes that account for the intensity, direction and persistence of effort towards a goal.

    Content theories: what motivates

    Theory Key idea Evidence and use
    Maslow’s hierarchy of needs (1943)Five levels: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation; later versions add cognitive, aesthetic and transcendence needsIntuitive, but research gives little support for a strict order in which each level must be satisfied before the next. Useful to explain why job security matters most to low-paid or insecure workers
    Alderfer’s ERG theory (1969)Three needs: Existence, Relatedness, Growth; several can operate at once; frustration of a higher need leads to regression to a lower oneA more flexible version of Maslow
    Herzberg’s two-factor theory (1959)Hygiene factors (pay, policies, supervision, conditions, security) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, growth) create satisfactionExplains why fixing pay stops complaints but does not create engagement; basis for job enrichment
    McClelland’s acquired needsNeeds for achievement, power and affiliation, shaped by experienceHigh achievement need suits entrepreneurial and sales roles; a socialised need for power suits leadership

    CorrectionThe compendium (p. 70) states that each of Maslow’s levels must be satisfied before the next. Research does not support a strict sequence; people pursue several needs at the same time. Present the hierarchy as a broad prioritisation.

    Process theories: how motivation works

    Theory Key idea HR application
    Vroom’s expectancy theory (1964)Motivation = Expectancy (effort will lead to performance) × Instrumentality (performance will lead to reward) × Valence (value of the reward). If any element is zero, motivation is zeroIncentive design: achievable targets, reliable payout, rewards people value
    Adams’ equity theory (1963, 1965)People compare the ratio of their outcomes (pay, recognition) to inputs (effort, skills) with that of others; perceived inequity leads them to reduce effort, seek more, change their comparison or leavePay compression, internal equity, transparent pay decisions
    Goal-setting theory (Locke and Latham)Specific, challenging goals with commitment and feedback raise performanceSMART goals, OKRs (Chapter 3)
    Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan)Intrinsic motivation depends on three needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness; controlling rewards can undermine intrinsic motivationAutonomy in how work is done, development, team connection
    Reinforcement theory (Skinner)Behaviour followed by positive consequences is repeated. Positive reinforcement adds a desirable consequence; negative reinforcement removes an undesirable one; punishment applies an undesirable consequence; extinction withholds reinforcementRecognition schemes, safety behaviour programmes

    Daniel Pink’s Drive (2009) popularised the argument, based on self-determination research, that for complex, creative work, autonomy, mastery and purpose motivate more than contingent rewards.

    Worked example (expectancy). A plant offers ₹2,000 for each month with zero rejected products. If operators believe the target is impossible with current machines, expectancy is near zero; if they believe payouts will be “adjusted” later, instrumentality is near zero. Either way the scheme fails, regardless of the amount.

    7.6Groups and teams

    In short

    Teams develop through stages, need complementary roles, and perform best when members feel safe to speak up and contributions are visible.

    Recall

    • Group vs team
    • Tuckman’s five stages
    • Belbin’s nine roles
    • Social loafing
    • Psychological safety
    • Virtual teams

    A group is two or more people who interact to achieve shared objectives; a team is a group whose members’ efforts produce collective results greater than the sum of individual inputs, with shared accountability.

    Tuckman’s stages (1965; the fifth added 1977): forming (orientation, politeness), storming (conflict over roles and approaches), norming (agreed norms and cohesion), performing (effective work) and adjourning (completion and disbanding). New project teams struggle in the early stages; facilitation and clear roles shorten them.

    Belbin’s team roles. Meredith Belbin identified nine roles people tend to play: Plant (creative), Resource Investigator (explores opportunities and contacts), Co-ordinator (clarifies goals, delegates), Shaper (drives action), Monitor Evaluator (critical analysis), Teamworker (supports cohesion), Implementer (turns ideas into plans), Completer Finisher (attention to detail) and Specialist (expert knowledge). Balanced teams cover the necessary roles.

    Social loafing is the tendency to exert less effort when working in a group than alone, first observed by Max Ringelmann in rope-pulling experiments. It is reduced by making individual contributions identifiable, keeping teams small and setting shared goals.

    Team effectiveness depends on clear goals, complementary skills, appropriate size, mutual accountability and psychological safety (Section 7.11). Virtual and hybrid teams need deliberate communication norms, trust-building and attention to fairness between remote and co-located members.

    7.7Conflict and negotiation

    In short

    Conflict can be functional or dysfunctional. The Thomas–Kilmann model describes five conflict-handling modes; principled negotiation focuses on interests and alternatives.

    Recall

    • Functional vs dysfunctional; task vs relationship conflict
    • Thomas–Kilmann five modes
    • Distributive vs integrative
    • Fisher & Ury: four principles
    • BATNA

    Conflict arises when one party perceives that another has negatively affected, or is about to affect, something it cares about. Task conflict (about the work) at moderate levels can improve decisions; relationship conflict (personal) is usually harmful.

    Thomas–Kilmann conflict modes

    Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann classified conflict-handling styles on two dimensions, assertiveness (satisfying one’s own concerns) and cooperativeness (satisfying the other party’s concerns):

    Mode Assertive Cooperative Appropriate when
    CompetingHighLowQuick action is vital; matters of safety or ethics
    CollaboratingHighHighBoth sides’ concerns are too important to compromise; long-term relationships
    CompromisingModerateModerateGoals are moderately important; time pressure; equal power
    AvoidingLowLowThe issue is trivial; a cooling-off period is needed
    AccommodatingLowHighThe issue matters more to the other party; preserving the relationship

    No mode is best in all situations; skilled managers choose the mode to suit the issue.

    Negotiation

    Distributive negotiation divides a fixed amount (one side’s gain is the other’s loss); integrative negotiation seeks solutions that create value for both (Chapter 10 applies this to collective bargaining).

    Roger Fisher and William Ury’s Getting to Yes (1981) set out principled negotiation: separate the people from the problem; focus on interests, not positions; invent options for mutual gain; and insist on objective criteria. Each party should know its BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, which sets the point below which it should walk away.

    Example. In a salary negotiation, the candidate’s position is “₹22 lakh”; the underlying interests may be compensating for an unvested bonus and a long commute. A joining bonus and a hybrid arrangement may meet those interests within the employer’s pay range.

    7.8Power and influence

    In short

    Power is the capacity to influence others. Its sources range from formal position to expertise and personal regard; influence tactics vary in effectiveness.

    Recall

    • French & Raven: legitimate, reward, coercive, expert, referent (+ information)
    • Influence tactics
    • Organisational politics

    John French and Bertram Raven (1959) identified five bases of power, to which Raven later added information:

    Base Source Example
    LegitimateFormal positionA plant head’s authority to approve shifts
    RewardAbility to give rewardsA manager deciding increments
    CoerciveAbility to punishDisciplinary authority
    ExpertKnowledge and skillsA compensation specialist’s influence over pay design
    ReferentAdmiration and identificationA respected senior colleague
    InformationAccess to and control of informationAn HR business partner who understands business priorities

    Personal power (expert and referent) tends to produce commitment; position power used coercively tends to produce compliance or resistance. Effective influence tactics include rational persuasion, inspirational appeals and consultation; pressure and legitimating tactics are less effective. Organisational politics, the use of influence to pursue self-interest, increases when resources are scarce, criteria are unclear and trust is low; transparent processes reduce it.

    7.9Leadership

    In short

    Leadership theories moved from traits to behaviours, then to situational fit, then to transformational and relational approaches. Effective leaders adapt their style and attend to both tasks and people.

    Recall

    • Trait theory
    • Ohio State, Michigan, Blake–Mouton
    • Fiedler; Hersey–Blanchard; path–goal
    • Transformational (4 Is) vs transactional
    • Servant, authentic, LMX, Level 5
    • Leadership vs management (Kotter)
    Approach Key idea Main contributors
    TraitEffective leaders share traits such as drive, integrity, self-confidence, intelligence and extraversionEarly research; traits predict who emerges as a leader better than who is effective
    BehaviouralLeadership is defined by behaviour: initiating structure (task focus) and consideration (people focus)Ohio State and Michigan studies; Blake and Mouton’s Managerial Grid (concern for production vs people)
    Fiedler’s contingency modelEffectiveness depends on matching the leader’s fixed style (task or relationship orientation, measured by the least-preferred co-worker scale) to the situation (leader–member relations, task structure, position power)Fred Fiedler
    Situational leadershipLeaders adapt style to followers’ readiness: directing, coaching, supporting, delegatingPaul Hersey and Ken Blanchard
    Path–goal theoryLeaders clarify paths to goals and remove obstacles, choosing directive, supportive, participative or achievement-oriented behaviourRobert House
    TransactionalExchange of rewards for performance; correction of deviationsSuits routine, measurable work
    TransformationalInspires followers to exceed self-interest through the “4 Is”: idealised influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualised considerationJames MacGregor Burns; Bernard Bass
    Servant leadershipLeader’s priority is serving followers’ growth and well-beingRobert Greenleaf
    Authentic leadershipSelf-awareness, transparency, consistency with values
    Leader–member exchange (LMX)Leaders form high-quality relationships with an “in-group” and more formal ones with an “out-group”, affecting performance and engagementExplains perceived favouritism within teams
    Level 5 leadershipCombination of personal humility and professional willJim Collins, Good to Great

    Leadership and management. John Kotter distinguished management, which copes with complexity through planning, budgeting, organising and controlling, from leadership, which copes with change through setting direction, aligning people and motivating them. Organisations need both.

    Example. Satya Nadella’s leadership at Microsoft from 2014 is often used to illustrate transformational leadership: a new direction (cloud first), and a culture reset from “know-it-alls” to “learn-it-alls”, reinforced by changes in performance management.

    7.10Organisational culture

    In short

    Culture is the shared assumptions, values and norms that shape how work is done. Schein’s levels explain its depth, the Competing Values Framework classifies it, and Hofstede explains national influences.

    Recall

    • Schein: artifacts, espoused values, basic assumptions
    • Embedding mechanisms
    • CVF: clan, adhocracy, market, hierarchy; OCAI
    • Hofstede’s six dimensions; India’s profile
    • Strong vs weak culture
    • Culture change

    Definition. Organisational culture is the pattern of shared basic assumptions, values and norms that a group has learned and that guides how members perceive, think and behave. It shows in what is rewarded, tolerated and talked about.

    Schein’s three levels

    Edgar Schein described culture at three levels of depth:

    Level Description Example
    ArtifactsVisible structures, processes, language, dress, office layout, rituals and stories; easy to see, hard to interpretOpen-plan offices; informal dress; first-name terms with leaders
    Espoused valuesStated strategies, goals, values and philosophies“We value innovation” in the values statement
    Basic underlying assumptionsUnconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs that actually drive behaviour“Mistakes are punished”, “the boss is always right”

    When espoused values conflict with basic assumptions, people follow the assumptions. A company that espouses innovation but punishes failed experiments will not innovate.

    How leaders embed culture. Schein identified primary mechanisms: what leaders pay attention to, measure and control; how they react to critical incidents and crises; how they allocate resources; what they role-model and teach; how they allocate rewards and status; and how they recruit, select, promote and remove people. These matter more than statements or posters.

    Competing Values Framework

    Kim Cameron and Robert Quinn’s Competing Values Framework classifies culture on two dimensions: internal focus versus external focus, and flexibility versus stability.

    Flexibility and discretion Stability and control
    Internal focusClan: collaborative, family-like; leaders as mentors; success through commitment, morale and developmentHierarchy: structured, rule-based; leaders as coordinators; success through reliability and efficiency
    External focusAdhocracy: creative, entrepreneurial; leaders as innovators; success through new products and growthMarket: competitive, results-oriented; leaders as hard drivers; success through market share and targets

    Organisations combine all four in different proportions. The Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument (OCAI) measures the current and preferred profiles, and the gap guides culture change. Typical tendencies: a cement plant leans towards hierarchy (safety and process), a sales-led FMCG company towards market, a start-up towards adhocracy, and a family-owned mid-sized firm towards clan.

    National culture: Hofstede’s dimensions

    Geert Hofstede’s research, originally on IBM employees in the 1960s and 1970s and later extended, describes six dimensions of national culture.

    Dimension Meaning India (commonly cited score) Implication for HR in India
    Power distanceAcceptance of unequal distribution of powerHigh (≈ 77)Deference to seniority; open upward feedback and 360s need anonymity and leader role-modelling
    Individualism vs collectivismFocus on self vs in-groupIntermediate (≈ 48): collectivist towards in-groups such as family and team, individualist in career ambitionLoyalty to the manager; strong role of referrals and family in career decisions
    Masculinity vs femininityCompetition and achievement vs care and quality of lifeModerately masculine (≈ 56)Visible success, titles and status matter
    Uncertainty avoidanceDiscomfort with ambiguityLow to moderate (≈ 40)Tolerance of ambiguity and improvisation; rules applied flexibly
    Long-term vs short-term orientationFuture-oriented persistence vs tradition and quick resultsIntermediate (≈ 51)
    Indulgence vs restraintGratification of desires vs control by normsRestrained (≈ 26)

    Caveats. The data are old, scores have been revised in later editions, the dimensions describe national averages rather than individuals, and India’s regional, generational and urban–rural diversity is large. Use the dimensions to explain why practices imported from other countries need adaptation, not to stereotype individuals.

    Strong and weak cultures; culture change

    A strong culture has widely shared and intensely held values; it guides behaviour without many rules but can resist necessary change. Culture change requires changing the experiences people have: what leaders model, measure and reward, whom the organisation hires and promotes, how decisions are made, and how failures are handled. It takes years, not months.

    Examples. Netflix’s “freedom and responsibility” culture (high performance, candour, few rules); Microsoft’s growth-mindset culture after 2014; the Tata group’s emphasis on trust and community, codified in the Tata Code of Conduct.

    7.11Psychological safety and DEIB

    In short

    Psychological safety lets people speak up and learn from mistakes; it is the strongest predictor of team effectiveness found in Google’s research. Diversity, equity, inclusion and belonging require both legal compliance and everyday practice.

    Recall

    • Edmondson (1999)
    • Project Aristotle (2012–15)
    • How leaders build safety
    • DEIB definitions
    • Indian law and data
    • Inclusion practices

    Psychological safety (Amy Edmondson, 1999) is a shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking: people can ask questions, admit mistakes, raise concerns and challenge ideas without fear of embarrassment or punishment. Google’s Project Aristotle (2012–15) studied around 180 teams and found psychological safety to be the most important of five factors in team effectiveness, followed by dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact.

    How leaders build it: framing work as learning, admitting their own mistakes, asking for input before giving their view, responding to bad news with curiosity rather than blame, and holding blameless reviews of incidents (including near-miss reporting in plants).

    DEIB

    Concept Meaning
    DiversityThe presence of differences in the workforce: gender, age, disability, caste, region, language, religion, sexual orientation, background, thinking style
    EquityFair access to opportunities, achieved by removing barriers that affect some groups more than others
    InclusionAn environment in which everyone can participate and contribute fully
    BelongingIndividuals’ feeling that they are accepted and valued as part of the organisation

    India context

    • Women: female labour-force participation has risen to about 42% (Periodic Labour Force Survey 2023–24) from about 23% in 2017–18, but representation in corporate middle and senior management remains low. The Companies Act, 2013 requires at least one woman director on the boards of listed and certain large companies. Maternity benefit, crèche requirements, the POSH Act and safeguards for women’s night work shape employment practice (Chapter 11).
    • Persons with disabilities: the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 requires equal-opportunity policies and reasonable accommodation by private employers.
    • Transgender persons: the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 prohibits discrimination in employment.
    • Disclosure: SEBI’s Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report requires the largest listed companies to disclose workforce diversity and wages paid to women.
    • Indian diversity also includes caste, region, language, rural–urban background and first-generation graduates, dimensions often missing from Western frameworks.

    Inclusion practices: structured and diverse hiring panels; pay-equity audits; transparent promotion criteria; flexible work; returnship programmes for women restarting careers; accessible workplaces and technology; employee resource groups; mentoring and sponsorship for under-represented groups; inclusive leadership training; and measuring inclusion through survey items, not only representation.

    7.12Interview questions

    In short

    Name the theory briefly, then apply it to the situation in the question.

    Herzberg says pay is a hygiene factor. Should organisations stop worrying about pay?

    No. Hygiene factors must be adequate, or people become dissatisfied and leave. But raising pay above what is seen as fair rarely creates engagement; motivators such as achievement, recognition, responsibility and growth do. Equity theory adds that perceived fairness relative to others matters more than the absolute amount.

    How would you change an organisation’s culture?

    Diagnose it first, using Schein’s levels and the Competing Values Framework to compare current and preferred cultures. Then change the experiences that form basic assumptions: what leaders pay attention to, measure, reward and model; hiring and promotion criteria; how mistakes are handled; and how decisions are made. Align systems such as performance management and recognition, tell stories of the new behaviour, and expect the change to take years.

    Which leadership style would you use with a team of new management trainees?

    Situational leadership suggests starting with directing and coaching, because trainees are enthusiastic but inexperienced, and moving to supporting and delegating as their competence grows. Self-determination theory adds that giving autonomy within clear goals, opportunities to build competence and team connection sustains motivation.

    What is psychological safety, and how would you know if a team lacks it?

    It is the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Signs that it is lacking: people do not raise problems in meetings, bad news reaches leaders late, near-misses are not reported and only the most senior person speaks. It can be measured with survey items such as “If you make a mistake on this team, it is often held against you.”

    Which bias concerns you most in hiring, and how would you control it?

    Similarity or affinity bias, which leads interviewers to favour candidates from their own college, region or background. Controls: structured interviews with anchored scoring, diverse panels, independent ratings before discussion and monitoring of selection rates by group.
