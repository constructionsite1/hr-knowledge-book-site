    Organisation Design, Job Architecture and Workforce Planning

    Chapter 6 · Organisation Design and Workforce Planning

    What this chapter covers. How strategy shapes organisation structure; the parameters of organisation design; the main structure types and when each fits; spans of control and layers; job design; job architecture and competency frameworks; workforce planning, including demand and supply forecasting and Markov analysis; succession planning; the HR operating model; and trends in the future of work.

    Why it matters for interviews. Questions on structure (functional, divisional, matrix), spans and layers, job architecture and manpower planning are common in HR and consulting interviews.

    6.1Strategy and structure

    In short

    Organisation design is the deliberate shaping of structure, processes, rewards and people so that strategy can be executed. Structure follows strategy, and all elements must align.

    Recall

    • Chandler (1962)
    • Galbraith’s Star Model
    • Mintzberg’s configurations
    • Mechanistic vs organic (Burns & Stalker)

    Definition. Organisation design is the process of deciding how work is divided, grouped, coordinated and governed, and aligning structure with processes, reward systems and people practices, so that an organisation can execute its strategy.

    Structure follows strategy. Alfred Chandler’s study of large American companies (Strategy and Structure, 1962) showed that as companies diversified into new products and markets, their centralised functional structures became overloaded and they moved to divisional structures. The principle is that structure should be chosen to fit the strategy, not inherited.

    Galbraith’s Star Model. Jay Galbraith described five design policies that must be aligned: strategy (direction), structure (where decision power sits and how units are grouped), processes (how information and decisions flow across units), rewards (what behaviour is measured and paid for) and people (the skills and mindsets the design requires). A common failure is to change structure alone: for example, forming cross-functional teams while still rewarding functional targets.

    Mintzberg’s configurations. Henry Mintzberg (1979) described five typical structural configurations, each relying on a different coordination mechanism.

    Configuration Coordination mechanism Typical example
    Simple structureDirect supervision by the owner or leaderStart-up, small family business
    Machine bureaucracyStandardisation of work processesCement plant, mass manufacturing, large bank operations
    Professional bureaucracyStandardisation of skills (professional training)Hospital, university, law or audit firm
    Divisionalised formStandardisation of outputs (targets for each division)Diversified conglomerate
    AdhocracyMutual adjustment among expertsConsulting projects, R&D, product development

    Mechanistic and organic structures. Tom Burns and G. M. Stalker (1961) found that mechanistic structures (centralised, formal, specialised, hierarchical) suit stable environments, while organic structures (decentralised, flexible, networked) suit changing environments. Most large organisations combine both: a mechanistic operations core and organic units for innovation.

    6.2Design parameters

    In short

    Every structure is a combination of six choices: specialisation, grouping, chain of command, span of control, centralisation and formalisation.

    Recall

    • Work specialisation
    • Departmentalisation
    • Chain of command; unity of command
    • Span of control
    • Centralisation
    • Formalisation
    Parameter Definition Trade-off
    Work specialisationHow narrowly tasks are divided into separate jobsEfficiency and expertise versus monotony and coordination costs
    DepartmentalisationHow jobs are grouped: by function, product, geography, customer or processExpertise versus market focus (Section 6.3)
    Chain of commandLines of authority from top to bottom; unity of command means each person reports to one bossClarity versus flexibility (matrix structures break unity of command)
    Span of controlNumber of direct reports per managerWide spans are cheaper and faster; narrow spans allow closer support (Section 6.5)
    CentralisationWhere decisions are madeControl and consistency versus speed and local responsiveness
    FormalisationDegree to which work is governed by written rules and proceduresPredictability and safety versus adaptability

    6.3Structure types

    In short

    The basic choices are functional, divisional and matrix structures. Newer forms (front–back, network, team-based) combine or loosen them. No structure is best in general; fit to strategy decides.

    Recall

    • Functional
    • Divisional: product, geography, customer
    • Matrix: weak, balanced, strong
    • Front–back hybrid
    • Network
    • Team-based / agile
    • HR implications

    Functional structure

    Employees are grouped by specialised function (manufacturing, sales, finance, HR), each led by a functional head reporting to the chief executive. Strengths: depth of expertise, economies of scale, standard policies and clear career paths within functions. Weaknesses: silos, slow cross-functional decisions, and no single owner of a product or customer below the CEO. Fits: single-business organisations with a stable product range competing on efficiency, such as a cement or steel company. HR implication: HR is usually centralised and designs common policies for the whole company.

    Divisional structure

    Employees are grouped into semi-autonomous divisions organised by product (consumer and industrial divisions), geography (north and south regions; India and international) or customer (retail and corporate banking). Each division has its own functional resources and usually its own profit and loss responsibility. Strengths: clear accountability for results, faster decisions close to the market, focus on specific customers. Weaknesses: duplication of functions and cost, inconsistent practices, missed synergies between divisions. Fits: multi-product or multi-market organisations. HR implication: HR business partners sit in divisions; a corporate HR centre sets common frameworks.

    Matrix structure

    Employees report along two dimensions at once, typically function (for expertise and standards) and business, product or region (for delivery), so many have two managers. A weak matrix gives most authority to functional managers; a balanced matrix shares it; a strong matrix gives most to project or business managers. Strengths: combines expertise with market focus and shares scarce specialists. Weaknesses: conflicting priorities between two bosses, slower decisions, unclear accountability, and a heavy demand on managers’ influencing skills. Fits: large, complex multinationals (Procter & Gamble, Unilever, Siemens) and project-based organisations. HR implication: clear decision rights (for example, a RACI matrix), joint goal-setting and performance input from both managers.

    Other forms

    Form Description Fits
    Front–backCustomer-facing “front” units organised by segment, supported by “back” units organised by function or product that serve all frontsBanks, telecom, IT services
    Network or virtualA small core organisation coordinating partners, contractors and suppliersAsset-light firms, platforms
    Team-based or agileCross-functional teams organised around products or customer journeys, with chapters or guilds maintaining functional skillsSoftware and digital units. The widely cited “Spotify model” of squads and tribes was, by Spotify’s own engineers’ account, never fully implemented as described, so use it as an idea rather than a template
    Flat structures and holacracyFew or no managerial layers; authority distributed to roles and circles (Zappos adopted holacracy in 2013–14)Small or experimental organisations; difficult to sustain at scale

    Indian examples

    Organisation Structural feature
    Hindustan UnileverFunctional backbone with “Winning in Many Indias” (WiMI), introduced around 2014, which divided India into about 14–15 consumer clusters with local decision rights on product mix, pricing and media: a geographic layer for responsiveness on top of scale efficiency
    Tata GroupHolding company (Tata Sons) with independent listed operating companies, each with its own board and management, linked by brand, values and group leadership development
    Aditya Birla Group, Mahindra GroupDiversified groups with largely autonomous businesses and group-level talent and governance
    Indian subsidiaries of multinationalsMatrix: a plant HR manager may report to the plant head (solid line) and the regional HR head (dotted line)

    In short

    The strategy defines the key success factors, and the key success factors indicate the structure.

    Recall

    • Differentiation → divisional or matrix
    • Focus
    • Diversification → holding / divisional
    Strategy (Porter) Key success factors Structure that fits Example

    DifferentiationInnovation, brand, customer intimacy, speed to marketDivisional or matrix, decentralised, cross-functional teamsPremium consumer brands, pharmaceutical R&D

    6.5Spans of control and layers

    In short

    Spans and layers determine cost and speed. The right span depends on the nature of the work, the team’s experience and the manager’s other duties.

    Recall

    • Factors affecting span
    • Typical 6–8; 10–15+ for routine work
    • Layers and delayering
    • Worked example

    Factors that allow a wider span: similar and routine work, an experienced team, good systems and procedures, co-location, and a manager with few non-managerial duties. Factors that require a narrower span: complex or novel work, inexperienced staff, dispersed teams, high risk (safety-critical work) and a manager who is also a major individual contributor.

    Typical values. Many corporate averages fall between six and eight direct reports; operational supervisors of routine work often manage 10–15 or more; senior leaders of complex functions may have fewer. Very narrow spans (fewer than four) are usually a sign of excess layers, or of managerial titles given for status.

    Layers are the number of levels from the chief executive to the front line. More layers slow decisions and communication and add cost. Delayering removes layers and widens spans.

    Worked example. A business unit has 1,200 employees and 240 managers, an average span of 5. Raising the average span to 7 in the parts of the unit where work is routine and teams are experienced would require about 1,200 ÷ 8 ≈ 150 managers (because each manager also counts in the total) instead of 240, freeing about 90 roles. In practice, redesign starts with the work (decision rights, systems), not with removing managers, and exits must follow the law (Chapter 10).

    6.6Job design

    In short

    Job design decides the content of jobs. The job characteristics model explains which features make jobs motivating.

    Recall

    • Specialisation vs enlargement, enrichment, rotation
    • Hackman & Oldham (1976)
    • Five core characteristics
    • MPS formula
    • Job crafting
    Approach Description Purpose
    SpecialisationNarrow, repetitive tasksEfficiency (Taylor’s scientific management)
    Job rotationMoving people across jobs periodicallySkills breadth, reduced monotony, cover for absences
    Job enlargementAdding more tasks at the same level (horizontal)Variety
    Job enrichmentAdding responsibility, autonomy and planning or control tasks (vertical), drawing on Herzberg’s motivatorsMotivation and ownership
    Job craftingEmployees reshape their own tasks, relationships and view of the jobEngagement and fit

    Job characteristics model

    J. Richard Hackman and Greg Oldham (1976) identified five core job characteristics: skill variety (range of skills used), task identity (completing a whole, identifiable piece of work), task significance (impact on others), autonomy (freedom in scheduling and method) and feedback (direct information about results). The first three create a sense of meaningfulness, autonomy creates responsibility, and feedback creates knowledge of results; together these lead to motivation, performance and satisfaction.

    Motivating potential score (MPS) = ((skill variety + task identity + task significance) ÷ 3) × autonomy × feedback. Because autonomy and feedback multiply the score, a job low on either has little motivating potential however varied it is.

    Application. Redesigning a dispatch clerk’s job so that one person handles a customer’s order from booking to delivery confirmation (task identity), with authority to resolve delays (autonomy) and a daily dashboard of on-time deliveries (feedback), raises its motivating potential.

    6.7Job architecture

    In short

    Job architecture is the framework that organises all jobs into families, levels and standard titles mapped to grades. It underpins pay, careers, mobility and workforce data.

    Recall

    • Job family, sub-family, track, level, title, grade
    • Level guides
    • Dual career ladder
    • Benefits
    • Building steps
    • Governance

    Definition. Job architecture is the structured framework that classifies all jobs in an organisation by job family (type of work) and level (size and complexity), with standard titles, linked to the grading and pay structure.

    Element Meaning Example
    Job familyBroad area of workHuman Resources
    Sub-familySpecialisation within the familyTalent Acquisition; Total Rewards; HR Business Partnering
    Career trackPath typeProfessional (individual contributor) or management
    LevelSize and complexity of the role, defined by a level guideP1 Associate, P2 Specialist, P3 Senior Specialist, P4 Lead; M1 Manager, M2 Senior Manager, M3 Head
    Standard titleConsistent name for the job“Senior Specialist, Talent Acquisition”
    GradePay grade from job evaluation (Chapter 2)Each level maps to a grade and pay range

    Level guides describe each level by generic criteria such as scope of impact, complexity of problems, autonomy, knowledge and leadership responsibility, so any job can be placed consistently.

    Dual career ladder. Organisations with deep technical work (R&D, engineering, technology) create a professional track that rises to senior levels equivalent to management grades, so experts can progress without managing people.

    Why organisations build job architecture

    Benefit How
    Accurate benchmarkingStandard jobs can be matched to survey jobs on content (Chapter 2)
    Internal equitySame level and grade means same pay range across units
    Career paths and mobilityEmployees can see routes within and across families
    Workforce planning and analyticsClean, comparable job data in the HRIS
    Mergers and integrationA common framework to map jobs from different organisations
    Title controlFewer, meaningful titles; less title inflation

    Building job architecture

    1. Collect job data through job analysis and existing job descriptions.
    2. Define job families and sub-families.
    3. Write level guides for each track.
    4. Map every job to a family, sub-family and level, using job evaluation where needed.
    5. Rationalise titles to a standard set (merging titles for the same work, splitting titles that cover different levels).
    6. Link levels to grades and pay ranges.
    7. Load into the HRIS and set governance: who approves new jobs, titles and re-levelling.
    8. Communicate, especially where titles change, because titles carry status.

    6.8Competency frameworks

    In short

    A competency framework defines the behaviours and capabilities needed for performance, at several proficiency levels. It connects selection, performance management, development and succession.

    Recall

    • Core, functional, leadership competencies
    • Definition + behavioural indicators + levels
    • Building: critical incidents, BEI, expert panels
    • Uses
    • Pitfalls

    Definition. A competency is an observable, measurable combination of knowledge, skills, abilities and behaviours that distinguishes effective performance in a role. A competency framework is the organised set of competencies for an organisation or job family.

    Type Applies to Example
    Core (organisational)All employees, linked to valuesIntegrity, customer focus, collaboration
    Functional or technicalA job familyCompensation analysis; process safety management
    LeadershipPeople managers, by levelDeveloping others, strategic thinking

    Structure of a competency. A definition, behavioural indicators and proficiency levels, for example: 1 Basic (applies with guidance), 2 Intermediate (applies independently), 3 Advanced (handles complex situations and guides others), 4 Expert (shapes practice across the organisation). Each role specifies the required level for each competency.

    How frameworks are built. Through job analysis and expert panels; the critical incident technique (examples of very effective and ineffective behaviour); and behavioural event interviews with high and average performers, an approach developed by David McClelland and later Lyle and Signe Spencer, which identifies the behaviours that distinguish superior performers. Many organisations adapt commercial libraries such as Korn Ferry’s (Lominger) competency library.

    Uses: competency-based interviews (Chapter 1), the “how” of performance (Chapter 3), training needs analysis (Chapter 4), succession and assessment centres (Section 6.10), and career paths.

    Pitfalls: too many competencies (more than eight to ten per role becomes unusable), vague definitions without behavioural indicators, frameworks that are never used in actual decisions, and generic frameworks copied without validation.

    6.9Workforce planning

    In short

    Workforce planning compares future demand for people and skills with future supply and plans how to close the gaps. It combines judgement with quantitative methods.

    Recall

    • Operational vs strategic WFP
    • Demand: judgement, Delphi, scenarios, ratio, trend, regression, work study
    • Supply: skills inventory, replacement charts, Markov
    • Gap analysis
    • 6 Bs

    Definition. Workforce planning (traditionally human resource planning, HRP) is the process of forecasting the organisation’s future demand for people and skills, assessing future supply, and planning actions to close the gaps, so that the right people with the right skills are available at the right time and cost.

    Horizons. Operational workforce planning covers the next 12–18 months, linked to the budget and headcount plan. Strategic workforce planning looks three to five years ahead, focusing on capabilities the strategy requires, such as digital or AI skills.

    Process

    1. Scan the environment and strategy: growth plans, new plants or markets, technology, regulation (for example, the Labour Codes), labour-market conditions.
    2. Forecast demand: how many people, with what skills, where and when.
    3. Forecast supply: the current workforce projected forward after attrition, retirements, promotions and transfers, plus the availability of external talent.
    4. Analyse gaps: shortages and surpluses by role, skill, level and location.
    5. Plan actions to close gaps (the 6 Bs below) and integrate them with recruitment, development and budgets.
    6. Monitor and adjust at least quarterly.

    Demand forecasting

    Method Type Description Example
    Managerial judgementQualitativeManagers estimate needs from business plansDepartment heads’ headcount requests
    Delphi techniqueQualitativeAnonymous experts give estimates over several rounds, seeing a summary each time, until they convergeForecasting demand for AI skills over five years
    Scenario planningQualitativePlans for several plausible futuresWorkforce needs under high, base and low growth
    Ratio analysisQuantitativeUses ratios between workload drivers and staff1 store manager, 2 shift leads and 25 pickers per dark store; 1 supervisor per 15 operators
    Trend analysisQuantitativeExtends past patterns forwardHeadcount growth over the past five years
    Regression analysisQuantitativeModels headcount as a function of business driversHeadcount = a + b × production volume
    Work studyQuantitativeCalculates staff from standard times for tasksOperators needed for a new packing line

    Ratio example. A quick-commerce company plans 120 new dark stores next year. At one store manager, two shift leads and 25 pickers per store, it needs 120 store managers, 240 shift leads and 3,000 pickers, before allowing for attrition. If annual picker attrition is 80%, keeping 3,000 positions filled requires about 2,400 additional hires during the year.

    Supply forecasting

    • Skills inventory: a database of employees’ skills, qualifications, experience and aspirations.
    • Replacement charts and succession plans: who could replace each key role holder, and when.
    • Markov analysis: a transition-probability matrix that projects how employees move between levels and out of the organisation.

    Markov example. Current workforce: 200 executives, 60 managers, 15 senior managers. Annual transition probabilities (from history):

    From \ To Executive Manager Senior manager Exit
    Executive0.750.1000.15
    Manager00.800.080.12
    Senior manager000.850.15

    Projected internal supply next year: executives 200 × 0.75 = 150; managers 60 × 0.80 + 200 × 0.10 = 68; senior managers 15 × 0.85 + 60 × 0.08 ≈ 18. If demand is 220 executives, 70 managers and 18 senior managers, the plan must hire about 70 executives and 2 managers externally, while senior-manager supply is sufficient. Markov analysis assumes past movement patterns continue, so it is adjusted for known changes.

    Closing gaps: the 6 Bs

    Option Meaning When appropriate
    BuildDevelop and reskill existing employeesTime is available; skills are firm-specific; retention value
    BuyHire externallyUrgent needs; new capabilities
    BorrowContractors, consultants, gig workers, fixed-term employeesTemporary or uncertain demand (fixed-term employment is now statutory, Chapter 10)
    BotAutomate the workRepetitive, rules-based tasks
    BindRetain critical peopleScarce skills with high flight risk
    BounceExit roles or people no longer neededSurplus; follow retrenchment law and consider VRS (Chapter 10)

    Challenges in workforce planning: uncertain forecasts beyond a year or two; poor data and inconsistent job information (one reason job architecture matters); weak links between business planning and HR; and resistance to the changes plans require.

    Examples. Amazon pledged in 2019 to spend about US$700 million to upskill 100,000 US employees by 2025, and in 2023 expanded the commitment to US$1.2 billion for more than 300,000 employees, through programmes such as Career Choice and Machine Learning University: a large-scale “build” strategy. Mastercard’s Unlocked internal talent marketplace, built on the Gloat platform, matches employees to projects, mentors and roles, supporting internal mobility.

    6.10Succession planning

    In short

    Succession planning prepares internal candidates for critical roles. Modern practice builds talent pools rather than naming one replacement per role.

    Recall

    • Critical roles
    • Success profile
    • Readiness: now, 1–2 years, 3+ years
    • Bench strength
    • Talent pools
    • Assessment and development

    Process.

    1. Identify critical roles: high impact on results and hard to fill, not only senior roles (for example, a kiln process specialist).
    2. Define success profiles: experiences, competencies and attributes needed.
    3. Assess potential successors through performance history, 9-box talent reviews (Chapter 3), assessment centres and 360-degree feedback.
    4. Classify readiness: ready now, ready in one to two years, ready in three or more years.
    5. Develop successors through stretch assignments, rotations, coaching and leadership programmes.
    6. Review at least annually in talent councils.

    Measures. Bench strength is the percentage of critical roles with at least one ready-now successor. Others include the internal fill rate for critical roles, the success rate of internal appointments and the diversity of succession slates.

    Talent pools. Because roles change and named successors leave, many organisations develop pools of high-potential people for families of roles, rather than one replacement for each position.

    6.11The HR operating model

    In short

    Ulrich’s model organises HR into business partners, centres of excellence and shared services. It improved HR’s strategic role but created hand-off problems; newer models add digital self-service and product thinking.

    Recall

    • Ulrich (1997): four roles
    • Three pillars
    • Criticisms
    • HR as a product; agile HR
    • HR-to-employee ratio

    Dave Ulrich’s model (Human Resource Champions, 1997) defined four HR roles: strategic partner (aligning HR with business strategy), change agent (leading transformation), administrative expert (efficient processes) and employee champion (representing employees’ needs). It was later applied as the “three-pillar” or “three-legged stool” operating model:

    Pillar Role Example
    HR business partners (HRBPs)Work with business leaders to translate business needs into people prioritiesHRBP for a business unit or region
    Centres of excellence (CoEs)Specialists who design policies and programmesTotal rewards, talent and leadership, learning, analytics
    Shared servicesEfficient, standardised transactions and queriesPayroll, onboarding administration, HR helpdesk

    Criticisms. Hand-offs between the three pillars confuse employees about whom to contact; HRBPs get pulled into transactional work; CoEs design programmes disconnected from business reality; and shared services can feel impersonal. Newer approaches add employee self-service and chatbots, treat HR offerings as products designed around employee experience, and use agile cross-pillar teams for priority projects.

    HR-to-employee ratio. The number of HR staff per 100 employees is a common efficiency benchmark; it varies widely with size, industry and the level of outsourcing and automation.

    6.13Interview questions

    In short

    Structure questions test trade-offs. Workforce questions test whether you can turn a business plan into numbers.

    Functional or divisional structure for a company entering three new product categories?

    It depends on how different the categories are. If they have different customers, channels and competitors, a divisional structure gives accountability and speed, with shared services for HR, finance and IT to avoid duplication. If they are similar, a functional structure with category managers, effectively a light matrix, keeps efficiency. The decision is a trade-off between responsiveness and economies of scale.

    What is job architecture, and why does it matter?

    It is the framework that organises all jobs into families, levels and standard titles linked to grades. It makes salary benchmarking accurate, supports internal equity, shows career paths and mobility, gives clean workforce data and provides a common framework when integrating acquisitions.

    How would you forecast manpower for a new cement grinding unit?

    Use ratios from existing units, such as people per tonne of capacity by department, adjusted for the new unit’s automation level. Add shift patterns and a leave reserve, then split the requirement into permanent, fixed-term and contract workers, checking contract-labour and standing-order thresholds. Plan supply through internal transfers for supervisors and local ITIs and apprenticeships for technicians.

    What problems does a matrix structure create, and how can HR reduce them?

    Conflicting priorities from two managers, slower decisions and unclear accountability. HR can reduce them by defining decision rights (for example with a RACI matrix), having both managers set goals jointly and contribute to performance reviews, providing forums to resolve conflicts, and developing managers’ ability to influence without direct authority.

    What determines the right span of control?

    The complexity and similarity of the work, the team’s experience, the quality of systems and procedures, geographic spread, and how much non-managerial work the manager carries. Routine work with experienced teams supports wide spans; complex, novel or safety-critical work needs narrower ones.
