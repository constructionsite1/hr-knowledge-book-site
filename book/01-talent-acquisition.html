    Talent Acquisition

    Chapter 1 · Talent Acquisition

    What this chapter covers. The difference between recruitment, selection and talent acquisition; the end-to-end hiring process; sourcing channels; the reliability and validity of selection methods and how to design structured interviews and assessment centres; fairness and legal requirements in selection; offer management and the Indian offer-to-joining gap; onboarding; campus, early-career and volume hiring; recruitment metrics; technology and AI in hiring; and policy developments in India that affect hiring.

    Why it matters for interviews. Questions on improving hiring quality, reducing offer drop-outs, measuring quality of hire and using AI in screening are common, especially for HR generalist, HRBP and TA roles.

    1.1Recruitment, selection and talent acquisition

    In short

    Recruitment attracts candidates, selection chooses among them, and talent acquisition is the long-term system that plans, attracts, selects and integrates the talent the business needs.

    Recall

    • Recruitment vs selection vs TA
    • ASA theory (Schneider, 1987)
    • Person–job fit, person–organisation fit
    • “Culture fit” risk

    Recruitment is the process of identifying and attracting a pool of suitable candidates for vacancies. Selection is the process of assessing candidates and choosing the one most likely to succeed. Talent acquisition (TA) is the broader, longer-term function that connects hiring to workforce planning: it includes employer branding, talent pipelines, sourcing strategy, selection, offer management, onboarding and the data to improve all of these.

    Recruitment Selection Talent acquisition
    Question answeredHow do we get enough suitable applicants?Who is most likely to succeed?How do we ensure the right talent is available when the business needs it?
    Time horizonCurrent vacanciesCurrent vacanciesOne to three years
    Main activitiesAdvertising, sourcing, referrals, agencies, campusTests, interviews, assessment centres, background checksWorkforce planning, employer brand, pipelines, internal mobility, analytics
    OutputApplicant poolHiring decisionCapability ready when required

    Fit and the ASA framework

    Selection looks at two kinds of fit. Person–job fit is the match between a person’s knowledge, skills and abilities and the requirements of the job. Person–organisation fit is the match between a person’s values and preferences and the organisation’s culture.

    Benjamin Schneider’s attraction–selection–attrition (ASA) framework (1987) explains how organisations become more homogeneous over time. People are attracted to organisations whose values resemble their own, organisations select people who fit, and people who do not fit leave (attrition). The result is a strong, consistent culture, but also a risk of sameness that limits diversity and adaptability. This is why “culture fit” as a hiring criterion can turn into hiring people similar to existing staff; many organisations now assess “culture add” or specific values instead.

    In short

    Hiring runs from requisition to onboarding. Most problems come from a poor intake discussion at the start, slow decisions in the middle, and neglect between offer and joining.

    1.3Sourcing channels

    In short

    Each channel has different cost, speed, quality and diversity effects. Referrals usually give the best quality and retention; internal hiring is the most underused channel.

    Recall

    • External: portals, careers site, campus, agencies, search, social, ITIs, staffing

    Passive candidates, people not actively looking, form most of the talent market for experienced roles. Reaching them requires direct sourcing, a credible employer brand and an offer that answers why they should move.

    Diversity sourcing. Broaden channels (women’s networks, returnship programmes for women restarting careers, disability employment partners, universities outside the usual list) and track the diversity of the candidate slate at each stage, not only of hires.

    1.4Selection methods: what predicts performance

    In short

    Good selection methods are reliable (consistent) and valid (predict performance). Structured interviews, job-knowledge tests and work samples have the strongest evidence; unstructured interviews and years of experience predict little.

    Recall

    • Reliability: test–retest, inter-rater
    • Validity: content, criterion, construct
    • Sackett et al. (2022) rankings
    • Structured interview design
    • STAR; behavioural vs situational
    • Assessment centres

    Two concepts from first principles

    Reliability is the consistency of a measure. A reliable test gives similar results when repeated (test–retest reliability), and a reliable interview produces similar ratings from different interviewers (inter-rater reliability). An unreliable method cannot be valid.

    Validity is the extent to which a method measures what it claims and predicts what matters. Content validity: the method samples the actual content of the job (a typing test for a typist). Criterion validity: scores correlate with a performance measure, either later (predictive) or at the same time for current employees (concurrent). Construct validity: the method measures the underlying trait it intends to measure, such as conscientiousness. Validity in selection is usually expressed as a correlation coefficient between method scores and job performance.

    Adverse impact occurs when a method selects members of a group at a substantially lower rate than others. In the United States, the “four-fifths rule” treats a selection rate for one group below 80% of the highest group’s rate as evidence of adverse impact; Indian law has no equivalent test, but the principle is useful for auditing fairness.

    Evidence on selection methods

    A 2022 re-analysis by Paul Sackett and colleagues corrected earlier estimates (Schmidt and Hunter, 1998) and produced the following approximate validities:

    Method Approximate validity What it means for practice
    Structured interviews0.42 (highest)Same job-related questions, anchored rating scales, trained interviewers
    Job-knowledge tests0.40Strong for technical roles
    Empirically keyed biodata0.38Rarely used in India
    Work-sample tests0.33Also give a realistic job preview
    Cognitive ability (aptitude) tests0.31Useful, but less dominant than earlier estimates suggested
    Integrity tests0.31Used in some financial and retail roles
    Personality (conscientiousness)About 0.19Adds some prediction; not a stand-alone method
    Unstructured interviews0.19Widely used despite weak prediction
    Years of job experienceAbout 0.1 or lowerExperience is not the same as performance

    Designing a structured interview

    1. Identify the competencies that matter for the role from job analysis (for example, stakeholder management, problem solving, resilience).
    2. Write questions for each competency, using two types. Behavioural questions ask about past behaviour: “Tell me about a time you had to persuade a sceptical line manager to adopt a new policy.” Situational questions ask about hypothetical situations: “What would you do if three operators refused overtime on a dispatch-critical day?”
    3. Create an anchored rating scale for each question, describing what poor, acceptable and strong answers contain (the same logic as BARS, Chapter 3).
    4. Train interviewers, assign competencies to panel members, and ask every candidate the same core questions.
    5. Rate independently before discussing, then discuss evidence against the anchors.

    Candidates answering behavioural questions are commonly coached to use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Interviewers can use the same structure to probe for missing parts, especially the candidate’s own actions and the measurable result.

    Other methods

    Method Description Use
    Aptitude testsNumerical, verbal and logical reasoningCampus and volume screening
    Personality questionnairesValidated instruments based on the Big Five or similar models (e.g., SHL OPQ, Hogan)Supplement interviews, especially for leadership; MBTI is not suitable for selection (Chapter 7)
    Situational judgement tests (SJTs)Candidates choose responses to realistic work dilemmasGraduate and frontline screening at scale
    Group discussionCandidates discuss a topic or caseCommon in Indian campus hiring; assesses communication and influence, but is hard to score reliably without structured observation
    Assessment centreSeveral exercises (in-tray or in-basket, role-play, case analysis, presentation, group exercise) observed by trained assessors against defined competenciesManagement trainee selection and promotion to leadership roles; high validity when well run, but costly
    Background verificationEducation, employment, address, criminal record checks, with consentStandard in Indian corporate hiring; important given known cases of fake experience certificates
    Reference checksConversations with previous managersMore useful when structured around specific competencies

    1.5Fair and lawful selection

    In short

    Fair selection reduces bias through structure and audits, and complies with Indian non-discrimination and data-protection law.

    Recall

    • Interviewer biases
    • Code on Wages: no gender discrimination in recruitment
    • RPwD Act; Transgender Persons Act
    • DPDP Act for candidate data
    • Appointment letters (OSH Code)

    Common interviewer biases (Chapter 7): first impressions and the halo effect, similarity or affinity bias (same college, region or background), contrast effects between candidates, stereotyping, confirmation bias after the first few minutes, and overconfidence in one’s ability to judge people quickly. Controls are structured interviews, diverse panels, independent scoring, and tracking selection rates by group.

    Legal requirements in India.

    Law Requirement for hiring
    Code on Wages, 2019No discrimination on the ground of gender, including transgender identity, in recruitment for the same or similar work
    Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016Non-discrimination; reasonable accommodation, including in the selection process (accessible tests, extra time where appropriate)
    Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019No discrimination in recruitment
    Maternity provisionsQuestions about marriage or pregnancy plans invite discrimination claims and should not be asked
    Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023Candidate data is personal data: collect only what is needed, give notice, obtain consent where required (for example, for background verification), keep it secure, and delete it when no longer needed (Chapter 9)
    OSH Code, 2020Appointment letters in the prescribed format are mandatory for every employee

    1.6Offers and the offer-to-joining gap

    In short

    In India, 60–90-day notice periods create a long gap between offer acceptance and joining, during which candidates receive counter-offers. Offer management and pre-boarding are therefore core TA activities.

    Recall

    • Notice periods 60–90 days
    • Counter-offers, multiple offers, renege
    • Offer-to-join ratio
    • Buyouts; joining bonus with clawback
    • Pre-boarding

    The problem. Notice periods of 60 to 90 days are normal in Indian corporate employment, and 90 days is common in IT services and consulting. During the notice period, the current employer may make a counter-offer, and other employers may make competing offers. Some candidates accept several offers and join only one. The result is a high offer drop-out or renege rate, which Indian TA teams track as the offer-to-join ratio.

    What works.

    Lever How it helps
    Faster decisions and approvalsReduces the time candidates spend comparing options
    Accurate, competitive first offerAvoids weak offers that invite counter-offers
    Clear explanation of the offerFixed, variable, benefits and expected take-home pay (Chapter 2, Section 2.10)
    Notice-period buyoutEmployer pays the candidate’s notice-period dues so they can join earlier, for critical roles
    Joining bonus with clawbackCompensates for forfeited bonus; repayable if the person leaves within 12–24 months
    Pre-boardingRegular contact from the hiring manager and team, early access to learning content, digital document completion, an assigned buddy
    Backup candidatesKeeping one or two strong finalists warm for critical roles

    The offer letter and appointment letter. An offer letter states the role, compensation, joining date and conditions (such as background verification). Under the OSH Code, an appointment letter in the prescribed format must be issued to every employee on joining.

    1.7Onboarding

    In short

    Onboarding integrates new hires so they become productive and committed. Most organisations handle compliance well and connection poorly.

    Recall

    • Bauer’s 4 Cs
    • Pre-boarding, induction, integration
    • 30-60-90-day plan
    • Buddy; manager role
    • Early attrition, time to productivity

    Definition. Onboarding is the process of integrating a new employee into the organisation, its culture and their role, from acceptance of the offer until they are fully productive, often six to twelve months.

    Talya Bauer’s 4 Cs (SHRM Foundation) describe four levels of onboarding:

    Level Covers Example
    CompliancePolicies, forms, legal requirementsDocumentation, POSH and safety training
    ClarificationUnderstanding of the job and expectations30-60-90-day goals agreed with the manager
    CultureNorms and values, formal and informalLeadership sessions; stories of how decisions are made
    ConnectionRelationships and networksBuddy, introductions to key stakeholders, team rituals

    Good practice: pre-boarding before day one; laptop, access and workspace ready on the first day; a manager-led first week; a buddy; a 30-60-90-day plan with early contribution; check-ins at 30, 60 and 90 days; and a short onboarding survey. Measures: early attrition, time to productivity, new-hire engagement and hiring-manager satisfaction.

    1.8Campus and early-career hiring

    In short

    Campus hiring builds a pipeline of trainable talent and shapes employer brand. Internships converting to pre-placement offers are the most reliable route.

    Recall

    • Campus process
    • PPOs from internships
    • Management trainee programmes
    • TCS NQT
    • Fresher onboarding delays (2022–24)

    Typical Indian campus process: pre-placement talk, online aptitude or domain test, group discussion or case exercise, one or more interviews (often including HR), offer. Many employers rely heavily on summer internships converting into pre-placement offers (PPOs), because an internship is an extended work sample.

    Early-career programmes. Management trainee programmes (for example HUL’s Unilever Future Leaders Programme, ITC’s management trainee programme, the Tata Administrative Service) rotate trainees across functions before a first posting. IT services companies hire freshers at scale through national tests such as the TCS National Qualifier Test (NQT) and train them before deployment.

    India contextBetween 2022 and 2024 several large IT services firms delayed onboarding freshers who had accepted offers, some for many months, as demand slowed. In 2025, Infosys let go several hundred trainees in Mysuru after they did not clear internal assessments. These episodes are used in discussions on campus offer ethics, workforce planning and the treatment of trainees.

    1.9Volume and frontline hiring

    In short

    High-volume frontline hiring is optimised for speed, accessibility and early retention rather than assessment depth.

    Recall

    • Mobile, vernacular applications
    • Same-day offers
    • Staffing partners
    • 30-day retention

    Retail, quick commerce, logistics, BPO and delivery roles can require thousands of hires a month with annual attrition above 50%. Effective volume hiring uses mobile-first and local-language applications (including WhatsApp-based chatbots), simple screening on essential criteria, walk-in or same-day interviews and offers, referral drives, and staffing partners for peaks. Key metrics are time-to-hire, show-up rates and retention at 30 and 90 days. Realistic job previews (showing actual shifts, pay and conditions before the offer) reduce early attrition.

    1.10Recruitment metrics

    In short

    TA is managed through efficiency metrics (time, cost), effectiveness metrics (quality of hire, retention) and experience metrics (candidate and hiring-manager satisfaction).

    Recall

    • Time-to-fill vs time-to-hire
    • Cost-per-hire
    • Yield ratios
    • Offer-acceptance, offer-to-join
    • Quality of hire
    • Early attrition
    • Source effectiveness
    Metric Formula or definition What it shows
    Time-to-fillDays from requisition approval (or job posting) to offer acceptanceSpeed of the whole process; for workforce planning. State which start point is used
    Time-to-hireDays from the candidate entering the process to accepting the offerRecruiter and process efficiency; candidate experience
    Cost-per-hire(External costs + internal costs) ÷ number of hires, as in the SHRM/ANSI standardEfficiency; compare across channels
    Selection (yield) ratioCandidates progressing to the next stage ÷ candidates at the current stageWhere the funnel leaks
    Offer-acceptance rateOffers accepted ÷ offers madeCompetitiveness of offers and employer brand
    Offer-to-join ratioCandidates who join ÷ offers acceptedIndia-specific drop-out risk
    Quality of hireComposite of first-year performance, time to productivity, hiring-manager satisfaction and 12-month retentionEffectiveness of selection; the most important and least measured metric
    Early attritionHires leaving within 90 or 365 days ÷ hires in that cohortHiring and onboarding quality
    Source of hire and source qualityShare of hires, and their quality and retention, by channelWhere to invest
    Candidate experienceSurvey at each stage, sometimes as a candidate Net Promoter ScoreEmployer brand and referrals
    Slate and hire diversityShare of under-represented groups at each stageWhere diverse candidates drop out

    Funnel example (campus HR trainees). 2,000 applications → 400 pass the aptitude test (20%) → 120 reach the case and group round (30%) → 40 are interviewed (33%) → 15 receive offers (38%) → 13 accept (87%) → 12 join (92%). To hire 24 next year at the same yields, about 4,000 applications are needed, or the yields must improve.

    Costs of hiring. Hard costs: job portals, agency and search fees, referral bonuses, campus travel, assessment tools, background verification, recruiter salaries. Hidden costs: interviewers’ time, vacancy costs (lost output, overtime), ramp-up time, and the cost of a bad hire, which for senior or critical roles can exceed a year’s salary.

    1.11Technology and AI in talent acquisition

    In short

    Technology speeds up hiring and improves data, but AI screening can reproduce bias and must be validated, audited and governed.

    Recall

    • ATS, CRM, assessment platforms
    • Chatbots, GenAI
    • Amazon tool scrapped (2018)
    • HireVue dropped facial analysis (2021)
    • NYC Local Law 144
    • Human in the loop
    Technology Function Risk
    Applicant tracking system (ATS)Manages requisitions, applications, workflow and dataOver-rigid keyword filtering
    Candidate relationship management (CRM)Maintains talent pools and pipelinesData-protection obligations for stored candidates
    Online assessmentsAptitude, coding, situational judgement, game-based testsValidity and accessibility must be checked
    ChatbotsAnswer questions, screen basic criteria, schedule interviews (for example, McDonald’s “Olivia” for hourly roles)Impersonal if overused
    AI screening and matchingRanks CVs or profiles against role requirementsLearns historic bias
    Generative AIDrafts JDs, outreach and interview guides; summarises interviewsAccuracy; candidates also use GenAI, reducing the signal from CVs and take-home tasks

    Cautionary cases. Amazon abandoned an experimental CV-screening tool in 2018 after finding it penalised CVs that included the word “women’s“, because it had learned from a decade of male-dominated hiring data. HireVue stopped using facial analysis in its video assessments in 2021 after criticism. New York City’s Local Law 144 (in force 2023) requires independent bias audits of automated employment decision tools.

    Governance principles: validate tools against actual job performance; audit outcomes by gender, age, region and language; keep a human responsible for rejection decisions; explain to candidates how they are assessed; and comply with the DPDP Act.

    1.12Case: Unilever’s graduate hiring

    In short

    Unilever replaced manual CV screening for graduate roles with online games, AI-scored video interviews and an assessment day, reporting faster hiring, cost savings and more diverse hires.

    Recall

    • ~250,000 applicants, ~800 roles
    • Pymetrics → HireVue → Discovery Centre
    • ~4 months → ~4 weeks
    • ~£1 million a year
    • Vendor-reported results

    Problem. Unilever’s Future Leaders Programme received about 250,000 applications a year for about 800 places, and the manual process took four to six months.

    Approach (from 2016). Candidates applied with their LinkedIn profiles; played Pymetrics neuroscience-based games measuring traits such as risk appetite and attention; completed a HireVue video interview scored with AI; and the shortlisted candidates attended a one-day Discovery Centre assessed by Unilever staff. Screening shifted from experience and university to potential, and every applicant received feedback.

    Reported results. Time-to-hire fell from about four months to about four weeks; recruiter screening time fell by about 75%; savings of about £1 million a year were reported; the share of hires from a wider range of universities rose; and candidate satisfaction was high.

    CorrectionThe compendium (p. 13) gives “7% reduction in recruitment time” and “$1 million saved in one year”. The widely reported figures are a reduction from about four months to about four weeks, and savings of about £1 million a year. These figures come largely from Unilever and its vendors, so describe them as “reported”; HireVue’s later removal of facial analysis (2021) is a useful balancing point.

    1.13Policy context in India

    In short

    Government schemes subsidise formal hiring and apprenticeships, and verification and fraud risks shape hiring processes.

    Recall

    • PM Viksit Bharat Rozgar Yojana (from Aug 2025)
    • Apprenticeships (NAPS, NATS)
    • PM Internship Scheme (2024)
    • Background verification
    • Employment-linked incentives. The Pradhan Mantri Viksit Bharat Rozgar Yojana, effective from 1 August 2025, provides an incentive to first-time employees registered with the EPFO and incentives to employers for additional formal hires, with longer support for manufacturing. It lowers the cost of hiring into formal, PF-covered employment.
    • Apprenticeships. The Apprentices Act, 1961 and the National Apprenticeship Promotion and Training Schemes (NAPS and NATS) share stipend costs, making apprenticeships a low-cost pipeline for manufacturing and services.
    • PM Internship Scheme (piloted from 2024) places young people in internships with large companies, with a government-funded stipend.
    • Verification and fraud. Fake experience certificates, proxy interviews and bribery in hiring are known risks. In 2023, TCS dismissed employees and barred staffing vendors after finding irregularities in its contractor hiring. Consent-based background verification and secure, proctored assessments are standard responses.

    1.14Interview questions

    In short

    Show that you know the evidence (structured interviews), the Indian context (notice periods, offer drop-outs) and the metrics.

    How would you improve the quality of hiring?

    Start with the selection method, because it has the largest effect: introduce structured interviews based on job analysis, with anchored rating scales, trained interviewers and independent scoring, and add work samples or job-knowledge tests where relevant. Measure quality of hire through first-year performance, time to productivity, hiring-manager satisfaction and 12-month retention, and compare across sources and interviewers to improve the process.

    How would you reduce offer drop-outs?

    Analyse where and why candidates drop out: by role, notice period and competitor. Speed up approvals, make accurate first offers and explain them clearly, use notice-period buyouts or joining bonuses with clawback for critical roles, run structured pre-boarding with the hiring manager involved, and keep backup candidates warm. Track the offer-to-join ratio monthly.

    Referrals are your best source. Is there any downside?

    Yes. Referrals tend to replicate the existing workforce, which reduces diversity, and can lead to favouritism. Track the diversity of referred candidates, set sourcing targets for under-represented groups through other channels, and apply the same structured assessment to referred and non-referred candidates.

    Would you use AI to screen 50,000 applications for frontline roles?

    Yes, for scale, with safeguards: validate the tool against the performance and retention of people actually hired, audit outcomes by gender, region and language, keep human review of borderline rejections, explain the process to candidates, and comply with the DPDP Act. The Amazon case shows what happens when a model learns from biased historical data.

    What is the difference between time-to-fill and time-to-hire?

    Time-to-fill measures days from the requisition being approved or posted to an offer being accepted, so it reflects the whole process and is used for workforce planning. Time-to-hire measures days from when a particular candidate enters the process to acceptance, so it reflects process efficiency and candidate experience.

Agency fee ranges and funnel figures are indicative.
