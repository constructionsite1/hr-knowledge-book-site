    Labour Codes II: Wages, Social Security, Working Conditions and POSH

    Chapter 11 · Employment Law

    What this chapter covers. Why India consolidated its labour laws and where implementation stands; the Code on Wages, 2019; the Code on Social Security, 2020, including maternity benefit, employee compensation and the new framework for gig and platform workers; the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020, including working hours, leave, women’s employment, contract labour and inter-state migrant workers; the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013 (POSH); other laws HR must know; and a compliance agenda. Industrial relations under the IR Code is covered in Chapter 10. Payroll calculations for PF, ESI, gratuity and bonus are worked through in Chapter 2, Part 2.

    Status (October 2026). All four Codes came into force on 21 November 2025. Final central rules were notified on 8 May 2026. States are notifying their own rules on different timelines, so details for a specific establishment depend on the state.

    11.1Why the Labour Codes, and where implementation stands

    In short

    Four Codes replaced 29 central laws to simplify definitions, registrations and compliance, extend coverage and modernise enforcement. They are in force; rules are still being completed at state level.

    Recall

    • Second National Commission on Labour (2002)
    • 29 laws → 4 Codes
    • Wages 4, IR 3, SS 9, OSH 13
    • 21 Nov 2025; central rules 8 May 2026
    • Concurrent subject: state rules

    The problem the Codes address. Before the Codes, India had over 40 central labour laws and many more state laws, enacted at different times since the 1920s. They used different definitions of “wages”, “employee” and “establishment”, different thresholds and separate registrations, returns and inspections. Coverage was patchy: minimum wages applied only to scheduled employments, and most social security applied only to the organised sector. Employers faced complex compliance, and many workers had little protection.

    The reform. The Second National Commission on Labour (2002) recommended grouping labour laws into a few functional codes. After consultations, Parliament passed the Code on Wages in 2019 and the Industrial Relations, Social Security, and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Codes in September 2020.

    Code Laws merged Main subject
    Code on Wages, 20194Minimum wages, payment of wages, bonus, equal remuneration
    Industrial Relations Code, 20203Unions, standing orders, disputes, strikes, retrenchment (Chapter 10)
    Code on Social Security, 20209PF, ESI, gratuity, maternity, employee compensation, unorganised, gig and platform workers
    OSH Code, 202013Safety, health, working hours, leave, welfare, contract labour, migrant workers

    Timeline

    Date Event
    2019Code on Wages passed
    September 2020IR, SS and OSH Codes passed
    21 November 2025All four Codes brought into force; 29 central laws repealed
    30–31 December 2025Draft central rules published for consultation
    16 March 2026Ministry of Labour FAQs clarifying points such as the wage definition
    8 May 2026Final central rules under all four Codes notified, with Model Standing Orders, 2026
    17 September 2026EPF wage ceiling raised to ₹25,000 under the Social Security Code
    2026 onwardsStates notify their own rules; transitional provisions continue earlier rules where not inconsistent

    Central rules and state rules. Labour is a concurrent subject. The central rules apply where the Central Government is the “appropriate government”, for example for central public-sector undertakings, banks, insurance companies, railways, ports and mines. Most private factories, offices and shops are governed by state rules of the state where they operate.

    CorrectionThe compendium’s description of current status (p. 121) predates the final central rules of 8 May 2026 and the EPF ceiling change of September 2026. The accurate position is that the Codes have been in force since November 2025, central rules were finalised in May 2026, and states are at different stages of notifying theirs.

    11.2Code on Wages, 2019

    In short

    The Code on Wages gives every employee a minimum wage, introduces a national floor wage, applies one definition of wages, regulates payment and deductions, and bars gender discrimination in pay.

    Recall

    • Merged 4 laws
    • Universal minimum wage
    • Floor wage
    • Wages definition and 50% rule
    • Payment by the 7th; F&F in 2 days
    • Deductions ≤ 50%; OT 2×
    • Inspector-cum-facilitator

    Laws merged: Payment of Wages Act, 1936; Minimum Wages Act, 1948; Payment of Bonus Act, 1965; Equal Remuneration Act, 1976.

    Coverage

    The Code applies to all employees in all establishments. Earlier, minimum wages applied only to “scheduled employments” listed by governments, leaving a large share of workers outside statutory protection, and the Payment of Wages Act applied only to employees below a wage ceiling (₹24,000 a month in its final form). Removing these limits is the Code’s main change.

    Definition of wages

    Wages include basic pay, dearness allowance and retaining allowance, and exclude items such as HRA, conveyance, statutory bonus, overtime, commission, employer PF and gratuity. If the excluded items exceed 50% of total remuneration, the excess is treated as wages. The worked example and its effects on salary structures are in Chapter 2, Section 2.12.

    Minimum wages and the floor wage

    Element Rule
    Who fixes minimum wagesThe appropriate government (mostly state governments)
    BasisSkill category (unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled, highly skilled), geographical area, and working conditions such as hazardous or underground work
    UnitUnder the central rules, a daily rate; the hourly rate is the daily rate divided by 8, and the monthly rate is the daily rate multiplied by 26
    RevisionAt intervals not exceeding five years, with periodic adjustment for cost of living (variable dearness allowance)
    Floor wageSet by the Central Government based on minimum living standards (food, clothing, housing and other factors); may differ by region; state minimum wages cannot be lower. Revised at intervals of up to five years. The statutory rate was still to be notified as of 2026; the earlier non-statutory national floor level was ₹178 a day (2019)

    Payment of wages

    Element Rule
    ModeCoin, currency notes, cheque or bank or electronic transfer; electronic payment is encouraged
    TimingDaily wages: end of the shift. Weekly: before the weekly holiday. Fortnightly: within 2 days of the period’s end. Monthly: by the 7th of the following month
    On leavingOn removal, dismissal, retrenchment or resignation: within 2 working days
    DeductionsOnly authorised deductions: fines (with conditions), absence, damage or loss caused by neglect, housing and amenities provided, recovery of advances and loans, income tax, PF and other statutory contributions, union dues with consent, and others listed. Total deductions cannot exceed 50% of wages in a wage period
    OvertimeAt least twice the ordinary rate of wages
    RecordsWage registers and wage slips in prescribed formats; electronic records permitted

    Bonus

    The statutory bonus provisions, previously in the Payment of Bonus Act, apply to establishments with 20 or more employees and to employees earning up to ₹21,000 a month: minimum 8.33% and maximum 20% of wages, calculated on ₹7,000 or the minimum wage, whichever is higher, payable within eight months of year-end. Under the central rules, if a contractor fails to pay the minimum bonus, the principal employer must pay it on written notice from the employees or their union (Chapter 2, Section 2.16).

    Equal remuneration

    The Code prohibits discrimination on the ground of gender, including transgender identity, in wages and in recruitment and conditions of employment for the same or similar work. The Equal Remuneration Act covered only men and women and focused mainly on wages.

    Enforcement and penalties

    Element Position
    Inspector-cum-facilitatorReplaces the inspector; advises employers on compliance as well as inspecting, and can give time to correct defaults before prosecution
    InspectionsWeb-based, randomised and risk-based schemes, reducing discretion
    PenaltiesPaying less than due: fine up to ₹50,000; repeat within five years: imprisonment up to three months and/or fine up to ₹1 lakh
    CompoundingMany first-time offences that are not punishable with imprisonment can be settled by paying a compounding amount

    11.3Code on Social Security, 2020

    In short

    The Social Security Code consolidates nine laws, keeps the contributory schemes (PF, ESI), employer liabilities (gratuity, maternity, employee compensation), and extends social security to unorganised, gig and platform workers.

    Recall

    • 9 laws merged
    • EPF 20+, ₹25,000 ceiling
    • ESI 10+, ₹21,000
    • Gratuity: FTE after 1 year
    • Maternity 26 weeks; crèche 50+
    • Employee compensation
    • Gig: 1–2% turnover, cap 5%
    • e-Shram; 90/120 days

    Laws merged: Employees’ Compensation Act, 1923; Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948; Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952; Employment Exchanges (Compulsory Notification of Vacancies) Act, 1959; Maternity Benefit Act, 1961; Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972; Cine-Workers Welfare Fund Act, 1981; Building and Other Construction Workers Welfare Cess Act, 1996; Unorganised Workers’ Social Security Act, 2008.

    Structure of social security under the Code

    Type Schemes Funded by
    Contributory social insuranceEPF, EPS and EDLI (EPFO); ESI (ESIC)Employer and employee contributions
    Employer liabilitiesGratuity; maternity benefit (where ESI does not apply); employee compensation for injury (where ESI does not apply)Employer
    Welfare funds and schemesBuilding and construction workers (cess of 1% of construction cost), cine workers, unorganised workers, gig and platform workersCess, aggregator contributions, government

    Provident fund and ESI

    EPF / EPS / EDLI ESI
    Applies toEstablishments with 20+ employeesEstablishments with 10+ employees
    Covered employeesMandatory for PF wages up to ₹25,000/month from 17 September 2026 (previously ₹15,000)Gross wages up to ₹21,000/month (₹25,000 for persons with disabilities)
    ContributionsEmployee 12%; employer 12% (8.33% to EPS on wages up to the ceiling, the rest to EPF), plus EDLI 0.5% and administration 0.5%Employee 0.75%; employer 3.25%
    BenefitsRetirement savings, pension, life insuranceMedical care, sickness, maternity, disablement and dependants’ benefits

    Contribution examples are in Chapter 2, Sections 2.13–2.14.

    Gratuity

    Payable on leaving after five years of continuous service (not required on death or disablement), at 15 days’ wages per completed year (formula: 15 × last wages × years ÷ 26), up to ₹20 lakh, within 30 days. Fixed-term employees become eligible after one year, on a pro-rata basis. Because “wages” now follows the uniform definition, gratuity rises where basic pay was low. See Chapter 2, Section 2.15.

    Maternity benefit

    Element Rule
    EligibilityAt least 80 days worked in the 12 months before the expected date of delivery
    Duration26 weeks for the first two children, of which up to 8 weeks may be before delivery; 12 weeks from the third child (up to 6 weeks before delivery)
    Adoption12 weeks for a woman who legally adopts a child below three months of age
    Commissioning mother (surrogacy)12 weeks from the date the child is handed over
    Miscarriage or medical termination6 weeks
    PaymentAverage daily wage for the period of absence; where ESI applies, ESI pays the maternity benefit
    Medical bonusPayable if the employer does not provide free pre-natal and post-natal care
    Work from homeMay be allowed after the leave period, by agreement, where the nature of work permits
    CrècheEstablishments with 50 or more employees must provide a crèche for children under six, within one kilometre under the central rules; mothers may visit four times a day. Where there is no negotiating union or council, a majority of employees may agree with the employer on a crèche allowance of at least ₹500 per child per month (up to two children) instead
    ProtectionNo dismissal or adverse change in conditions during maternity absence

    Cost and hiring bias. Outside ESI, the full cost of maternity benefit falls on the employer, which raises concerns that some employers may avoid hiring women. This is a frequent discussion point, and the main argument for social-insurance funding of maternity benefit.

    Employee compensation for injury

    Where ESI does not apply, the employer must pay compensation for personal injury caused by accident arising out of and in the course of employment, and for listed occupational diseases. Compensation depends on the nature of the injury, the worker’s monthly wages and age. Under the employee compensation provisions, compensation for death is 50% of monthly wages multiplied by an age-based “relevant factor”, subject to a minimum of ₹1.2 lakh; for permanent total disablement it is 60% of monthly wages multiplied by the relevant factor, subject to a minimum of ₹1.4 lakh. Employers usually insure this liability through workmen’s compensation insurance.

    Unorganised, gig and platform workers

    Element Position
    Unorganised workerA home-based, self-employed or wage worker in the unorganised sector, or a worker in the organised sector not covered by the main schemes
    Gig workerA person who performs work and earns from it outside a traditional employer–employee relationship
    Platform workerA person who accesses organisations or individuals through an online platform to provide services and earn payment, such as ride-hailing, delivery and quick-commerce workers
    Legal statusRecognised for social security for the first time; they are not employees under the Code
    FundingAggregators contribute 1–2% of annual turnover, capped at 5% of the amounts paid or payable to gig and platform workers, to a Social Security Fund; the rate is set by notification for each scheme
    RegistrationWorkers aged 16 and above self-register on the central portal (e-Shram) with Aadhaar. Aggregators had to upload existing workers within 45 days of the rules (deadline 21 June 2026) and must register new workers and exits in real time or daily through APIs
    Eligibility for benefitsEngagement for at least 90 days with one aggregator or 120 days across aggregators in the previous financial year, plus scheme-specific conditions
    Benefits plannedLife and disability cover, accident insurance, health and maternity benefits, old-age protection; the February 2025 Union Budget extended PM-JAY health cover to registered platform workers
    State lawsRajasthan’s Platform Based Gig Workers (Registration and Welfare) Act, 2023 was the first; Karnataka’s Platform-based Gig Workers (Social Security and Welfare) Act, 2025 creates a welfare fund financed by a fee on platforms and includes transparency and grievance provisions; platforms have challenged it in the High Court, which declined to stay it

    Key ideaThe Code creates a third category between employee and independent contractor: gig and platform workers have social-security rights funded largely by platforms, but not employee rights such as minimum wages, PF or ESI as of right. This is the centre of most debates on the gig economy.

    Other provisions

    • Employment information: employers must report vacancies, other than those excluded, to designated career centres before filling them, carrying forward the Employment Exchanges Act.
    • Aadhaar-based registration is used to identify beneficiaries and make benefits portable across states and employers.

    11.4Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020

    In short

    The OSH Code sets common rules for safety, health, welfare, working hours and leave across establishments, with a single registration. It introduced mandatory appointment letters and allowed women to work at night with safeguards.

    Recall

    • 13 laws merged
    • 10+ workers; factory 20/40
    • Single registration
    • Appointment letters
    • 8-hour day, 48-hour week
    • OT 2×, consent, 144 hrs/quarter
    • Annual leave: 180 days → 1 per 20
    • Women at night: consent + safeguards

    Laws merged include the Factories Act, 1948; Mines Act, 1952; Plantations Labour Act, 1951; Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970; Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act, 1979; Building and Other Construction Workers Act, 1996; Dock Workers Act, 1986; Working Journalists Act, 1955; Motor Transport Workers Act, 1961; Sales Promotion Employees Act, 1976; and Beedi and Cigar Workers Act, 1966, among others (13 in all).

    Coverage and registration

    Element Rule
    Establishments coveredThose employing 10 or more workers, and all mines and docks regardless of size
    FactoryPremises where a manufacturing process is carried on with 20 or more workers with power, or 40 or more without power (previously 10 and 20 under the Factories Act)
    RegistrationOne registration for an establishment, replacing multiple registrations under separate laws
    Licence for contractorsA single licence, which can be valid in more than one state or across India

    Employer duties

    • Provide a workplace free from hazards that cause or are likely to cause injury or occupational disease.
    • Issue an appointment letter to every employee, in the prescribed format, stating details such as designation, category, wages and social-security entitlements. This applies to contract and fixed-term workers as well.
    • Provide free annual health check-ups for specified workers; the central rules require them for workers aged 40 and above in docks, mines and construction, and states specify others.
    • Report accidents and dangerous occurrences; maintain records.
    • Provide welfare facilities: drinking water, toilets and washrooms, first aid, canteens, rest rooms, crèches and welfare officers, each above the thresholds set by the Code and rules.
    • Comply with national occupational safety and health standards, including those for hazardous processes, and set up safety committees and appoint safety officers where required.

    Working hours and leave

    Element Rule
    Daily hoursNormal working day of 8 hours (for daily-wage workers under the central rules)
    Weekly hoursNot more than 48 hours a week
    Spread-over and intervalsSet by rules; several states permit longer working days (for example, 12-hour days including breaks) within the 48-hour weekly limit, which makes compressed weeks possible
    OvertimeAt twice the ordinary rate, with the worker’s consent; under the central rules, no more than 144 hours in a quarter. For calculation, 15–30 minutes counts as half an hour and more than 30 minutes as one hour
    Weekly holidayAt least one day a week
    Annual leave with wagesWorkers who have worked 180 days or more in a calendar year earn one day of leave for every 20 days of work (the Factories Act required 240 days); limited carry-forward and encashment are allowed

    Women’s employment

    Women may be employed in all establishments for all types of work, including night shifts (before 6 a.m. and after 7 p.m.) and hazardous operations, subject to:

    • prior written consent of the woman;
    • safe transportation to and from the workplace;
    • adequate facilities, including well-lit washrooms and drinking water;
    • CCTV coverage of the workplace, including the routes to these facilities;
    • compliance with the POSH Act (Section 11.6);
    • no employment in violation of maternity provisions.

    11.5Contract labour and inter-state migrant workers

    In short

    Contract labour is regulated when 50 or more contract workers are engaged. The principal employer remains liable for wages and amenities if the contractor defaults, and contract labour is barred in core activities with exceptions.

    Recall

    • Principal employer vs contractor
    • Threshold 50 (was 20)
    • Single pan-India licence
    • Core activities bar + exceptions
    • Vicarious liability for wages
    • 2% annual increment
    • SAIL (2001): no automatic absorption
    • Migrant workers: ≤ ₹18,000, journey allowance

    Definitions. Contract labour are workers employed in an establishment through a contractor, who has the direct employment relationship with them. The principal employer is the owner or occupier of the establishment where they work.

    Changes from the CLRA Act

    Aspect Contract Labour (R&A) Act, 1970 OSH Code, 2020
    ApplicabilityEstablishments or contractors with 20+ contract workers50+ contract workers
    LicenceSeparate licences per establishment and stateOption of a single licence valid across states or India
    Core activitiesGovernment could prohibit contract labour in particular processes after examining criteriaContract labour prohibited in core activities, with exceptions: where such work is normally done through contractors in that industry, where the activity does not require full-time workers for most of the working hours, or where there is a sudden increase in work that must be completed in a specified time
    Contract workers regularly employed by contractorsNo specific provisionWorkers employed regularly by a contractor under agreed terms are excluded from the definition of contract labour; under the central rules they must receive an annual increment of at least 2%

    Principal employer’s obligations

    • Wage liability: the contractor pays wages, but if the contractor fails to pay in full or on time, the principal employer must pay and can recover the amount from the contractor (vicarious liability). The same applies to the minimum statutory bonus.
    • Amenities: where contract workers work on the principal employer’s premises, the principal employer provides basic facilities such as toilets, washrooms, drinking water, changing rooms, first aid, canteen and crèche; other entitlements are the contractor’s responsibility.
    • Grievances: contract workers’ grievances about health, working conditions or wages are addressed through a committee of the principal employer; unresolved issues go to the inspector-cum-facilitator after 30 days (central rules).

    Absorption and sham contracts. In Steel Authority of India v. National Union Waterfront Workers (2001), the Supreme Court held that prohibition of contract labour does not automatically make contract workers employees of the principal employer. However, where a contract is found to be a sham or camouflage, with the principal employer actually supervising and controlling the workers, courts can treat them as the principal employer’s employees.

    Why contract labour is an IR issue. Large pay and benefit gaps between permanent and contract workers doing similar work, and contractors’ failures to pay wages, were central to the Maruti Manesar (2012) and Wistron Kolar (2020) incidents (Chapter 10). HR in manufacturing therefore audits contractors’ wage payments, PF and ESI deposits monthly.

    Inter-state migrant workers

    The OSH Code covers inter-state migrant workers earning up to ₹18,000 a month, whether recruited by an employer or contractor or migrating on their own. Provisions include registration and a national database, an annual journey allowance to their home state, equal wages and conditions with local workers, a helpline, and portability of benefits such as public distribution (through One Nation One Ration Card) and building-workers’ welfare benefits. The COVID-19 lockdown in 2020, when large numbers of migrant workers were stranded, highlighted the absence of data and portable protection.

    11.6Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013

    In short

    The POSH Act requires every employer with 10 or more employees to have an Internal Committee and a time-bound process for complaints of sexual harassment against women. It remains a separate law outside the Codes.

    Recall

    • Vishaka (1997)
    • Aggrieved woman; wide workplace
    • Definition; impact not intent
    • IC at 10+; composition
    • Local Committee
    • 3 months; 90/10/60/90 days
    • Interim relief; confidentiality
    • ₹50,000 penalty
    • Aureliano Fernandes (2023)

    Origin. In Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997), after the gang-rape of Bhanwari Devi, a social worker, while she was trying to stop a child marriage, the Supreme Court laid down guidelines on preventing sexual harassment at work, treating it as a violation of fundamental rights. The POSH Act, 2013 gave these guidelines statutory form.

    Scope and definitions

    Element Meaning
    Aggrieved womanA woman of any age, whether employed or not, who alleges sexual harassment at a workplace. The Act protects women; men and other genders are protected only by company policies that go further
    EmployeeIncludes regular, temporary, ad hoc, daily-wage, contract and fixed-term workers, trainees, apprentices and interns, paid or unpaid
    WorkplaceBroad: includes any place visited by the employee in the course of employment, including transport provided by the employer, client sites, off-sites and, in practice, virtual workspaces
    Sexual harassmentUnwelcome acts or behaviour, directly or by implication: physical contact and advances; a demand or request for sexual favours; sexually coloured remarks; showing pornography; any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature. It also covers implied or explicit promises of preferential treatment, threats about employment, interference with work or creation of a hostile environment, and humiliating treatment likely to affect health or safety, when connected with such conduct
    TestWhether the conduct was unwelcome and its impact on the woman, not the intention of the respondent

    Committees

    Committee When Composition
    Internal Committee (IC)Every workplace with 10 or more employees, at each office or administrative unitPresiding Officer: a woman employed at a senior level. At least two members from among employees, committed to the cause of women or with experience in social work or legal knowledge. One external member from an NGO or association committed to women’s causes, or a person familiar with issues of sexual harassment. At least half the members must be women. Term up to three years
    Local Committee (LC)Constituted by the District Officer in every districtHandles complaints from establishments with fewer than 10 employees, complaints against the employer, and from domestic and unorganised-sector workers

    Complaint and inquiry process

    Stage Rule
    ComplaintIn writing, within 3 months of the incident (or the last incident in a series); the IC may extend by another 3 months for reasons recorded. The IC must help a woman who cannot write the complaint
    Notice to respondentCopy sent to the respondent within 7 working days; reply within 10 working days (rules)
    ConciliationOnly at the request of the aggrieved woman; no monetary settlement may be the basis of conciliation
    InquiryFollowing the service rules or, where none exist, the procedure in the rules, with principles of natural justice; complete within 90 days
    Interim reliefOn the woman’s written request during the inquiry: transfer of the woman or the respondent, leave for the woman of up to three months in addition to her entitlement, or restraining the respondent from reporting on her work
    ReportTo the employer within 10 days of completing the inquiry
    ActionEmployer acts on the recommendations within 60 days: action under service rules (warning to termination), and deduction from the respondent’s salary to pay compensation to the woman, determined considering mental trauma, loss of career opportunity, medical expenses, the respondent’s means and the feasibility of payment
    AppealWithin 90 days of the recommendations
    False or malicious complaintsAction only where the complaint is found malicious or knowingly false; failure to prove a complaint is not grounds for action
    ConfidentialityIdentity of parties, witnesses and proceedings may not be published or disclosed; breach attracts a penalty under the rules

    Employer duties and penalties

    Employers must: constitute the IC and display its details; adopt and display a POSH policy and the consequences of harassment; run awareness and training programmes, including orientation for IC members; provide facilities for the IC; assist in securing attendance of the respondent and witnesses; act on recommendations; treat sexual harassment as misconduct under service rules; and file an annual report with case details to the District Officer. Companies also disclose POSH case numbers in the board’s report under the Companies Act rules.

    Penalties: failure to constitute an IC or comply with the Act attracts a fine of up to ₹50,000; repeat offences attract double penalties and may lead to cancellation of the business licence or registration.

    India contextIn Aureliano Fernandes v. State of Goa (May 2023) the Supreme Court found serious gaps in the constitution of ICs across government bodies and institutions and directed that ICs be properly constituted and their details published. The government’s SHe-Box portal allows women to file complaints online and track them. Many companies extend their policies to all genders and to LGBTQ+ employees voluntarily.

    HR’s role in practice. Ensuring the IC is correctly constituted (including a genuinely independent external member and no members who report to likely respondents), training IC members in inquiry procedure, keeping proceedings confidential, protecting complainants and witnesses from retaliation, acting on findings regardless of the respondent’s seniority, and analysing cases for patterns.

    11.7Other laws HR must know

    In short

    Several laws remain outside the Codes and regularly appear in HR work.

    Recall

    • Apprentices Act, 1961
    • Shops & Establishments Acts
    • Child and Adolescent Labour Act
    • RPwD Act, 2016
    • Transgender Persons Act, 2019
    • DPDP Act, 2023
    Law Key provisions for HR
    Apprentices Act, 1961Apprentices are trainees under a registered contract of apprenticeship, paid a stipend, not wages; they are not workers under labour laws during training. Government support through the National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme (trades) and National Apprenticeship Training Scheme (graduates and diploma holders)
    State Shops and Establishments ActsRegistration, working hours, holidays, leave and conditions for shops, offices, IT companies and other commercial establishments; vary by state
    Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 (amended 2016)No employment of children below 14 in any occupation (except helping a family enterprise outside school hours, without affecting education); adolescents aged 14–18 may not work in hazardous occupations and processes
    Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016Non-discrimination; private establishments must publish an equal-opportunity policy and provide reasonable accommodation and accessibility; 4% reservation applies to government establishments
    Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019No discrimination in employment, recruitment or promotion; establishments must designate a complaint officer
    Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023Governs processing of employee and candidate personal data (Chapter 9)

    11.8What HR must do under the Codes

    In short

    Compliance under the Codes is a programme, not a single change: pay structure, contracts, records, committees, contractor management and state-wise tracking.

    Area Actions
    Pay structuresApply the uniform wage definition and the 50% rule; recompute PF, gratuity, bonus, overtime and leave encashment; communicate changes in take-home pay in advance
    EPFImplement the ₹25,000 ceiling from 17 September 2026 for newly covered employees and EPS contributions
    ContractsIssue appointment letters in the prescribed format to all employees, including contract and fixed-term staff; review fixed-term contracts for parity and gratuity
    Payroll and exitsPay wages by the 7th; complete full-and-final settlement within two working days; cap deductions at 50%
    Working hoursRevise shift policies within 8-hour and 48-hour limits; overtime consent, 2× pay and the quarterly cap
    Women at nightWritten consent, transport, CCTV and facilities
    Contract labourIdentify core activities; licences; monthly audit of contractors’ wages, PF and ESI; amenities; grievance committee; 2% increment for regular contract workers
    Gig workers (aggregators)Register workers on e-Shram via API; provision for the Social Security Fund contribution; comply with state laws
    CommitteesGrievance Redressal Committee (20+), Works Committee (100+), Internal Committee under POSH (10+), safety committees where required
    Records and returnsRegisters, wage slips and returns in the new formats, electronically where permitted
    State trackingMonitor state rules for every state where the organisation operates

    11.9Interview questions

    In short

    Expect factual questions on thresholds and timelines and judgement questions on gig work, contract labour and POSH cases.

    What are the four Labour Codes, and what is their current status?

    The Code on Wages, 2019 and the Industrial Relations, Social Security, and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Codes, 2020. They replaced 29 central laws and came into force on 21 November 2025. The central rules were notified on 8 May 2026, and states are notifying their own rules, so detailed requirements vary by state.

    What is the floor wage, and how does it differ from the minimum wage?

    The floor wage is a national base fixed by the Central Government on the basis of minimum living standards, possibly varying by region. Minimum wages are fixed by the appropriate government, mostly states, by skill level and area, and cannot be set below the floor wage. The statutory floor rate was still to be notified in 2026.

    How does the Social Security Code treat gig workers? Is it enough?

    It recognises gig and platform workers for social security for the first time, funds schemes through aggregator contributions of 1–2% of turnover capped at 5% of payments to workers, requires registration on e-Shram, and sets eligibility at 90 days with one aggregator or 120 days across several. It does not make them employees, so they have no minimum wage, PF or ESI as of right. Gaps include unsettled contribution rates and benefits and the lack of rules on algorithmic management and deactivation, which states such as Karnataka have begun to address.

    Can women work night shifts in a factory?

    Yes. Under the OSH Code women can work in all establishments, including at night and in hazardous work, with their prior written consent and with safeguards: safe transport, adequate facilities, CCTV coverage and compliance with the POSH Act.

    Walk me through a POSH complaint from receipt to closure.

    The woman files a written complaint within three months, extendable by three; the IC sends it to the respondent within seven working days. Conciliation is possible only at her request and cannot be based on money. Otherwise the IC conducts an inquiry following natural justice within 90 days, can recommend interim relief, and reports within 10 days. The employer acts within 60 days, and either party can appeal within 90 days. Confidentiality and protection from retaliation apply throughout.

    A senior leader who generates large revenue is accused of sexual harassment. How do you handle it?

    Exactly as any other case. Make sure no IC member reports to the respondent, bringing in additional external members if needed; offer interim relief to the complainant; keep the process confidential and protect her and the witnesses from retaliation; complete the inquiry in 90 days and act on the findings regardless of seniority; and inform the board’s oversight committee as appropriate. The reputational and legal damage from mishandling or concealing a case is far greater than losing one leader.

    In your plant, 45% of line workers are contract labour doing the same work as permanent workers for much less. What would you recommend?

    Assess legal risk: core-activity restrictions under the OSH Code and the possibility of the arrangement being treated as a sham. Assess IR risk, given cases like Maruti Manesar. Options include converting part of the contract workforce to fixed-term employment with pay parity, building a skills-based route from contract to permanent roles, enforcing wage and statutory compliance by contractors through monthly audits, and improving amenities. Present it as a phased plan with costs.
