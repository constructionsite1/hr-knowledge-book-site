    Industrial Relations and the Industrial Relations Code, 2020

    Chapter 10 · Industrial Relations

    What this chapter covers. The concepts and theories of industrial relations; the constitutional and legal foundations of Indian labour law; the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 and its key definitions; trade unions and union recognition; collective bargaining and settlements; standing orders; discipline and domestic enquiries; grievance handling; the dispute-resolution machinery; strikes and lockouts; lay-off, retrenchment and closure; fixed-term employment; workers’ participation; and lessons from major Indian industrial-relations cases. Wages, social security, safety, contract labour and POSH are covered in Chapter 11.

    Status of the law (October 2026). The four Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025, replacing 29 central laws. The final central rules, including the Industrial Relations (Central) Rules, 2026 and the Model Standing Orders, 2026, were notified on 8 May 2026. Labour is a concurrent subject, so states notify their own rules; thresholds and procedures can differ by state.

    Why it matters for interviews. Manufacturing, mining, infrastructure, public-sector and banking employers test industrial relations in depth, because plant HR roles involve unions, settlements, discipline and disputes every day.

    10.1What industrial relations is

    In short

    Industrial relations is the relationship between employers, workers and their unions, and the State, covering terms of employment, collective representation and the resolution of conflict.

    Recall

    • IR vs employee relations
    • Actors: employers, workers/unions, State
    • Unitary, pluralist, radical approaches
    • Dunlop’s systems theory
    • Where IR is strong in India

    Definition. Industrial relations (IR) is the system of relationships between employers, workers and their organisations (trade unions), and the State, through which terms and conditions of employment are set, collective interests are represented, and conflicts are resolved.

    Industrial relations and employee relations. IR traditionally refers to the collective relationship: unions, collective bargaining, disputes, strikes and settlements. Employee relations (ER) is broader and includes individual and non-union relationships: communication, grievances, discipline, fairness and well-being. In Indian IT and services, ER dominates; in manufacturing, mining, ports, banking, transport and the public sector, collective IR remains central.

    Theoretical approaches

    Approach View of the organisation and conflict Implication for practice
    UnitaryThe organisation is a single team with common goals; conflict is abnormal, caused by poor communication or troublemakersUnions are seen as unnecessary; emphasis on direct communication and loyalty. Common in new-economy firms
    PluralistThe organisation contains groups with different, legitimate interests; conflict is normal and should be managed through institutionsRecognised unions, collective bargaining, agreed procedures. The basis of Indian labour law
    Radical (Marxist)Conflict reflects a fundamental divide between capital and labour in societyExplains politically affiliated unions and the view of IR as a class struggle

    Dunlop’s systems theory (John Dunlop, 1958) describes an IR system as having three actors (management, workers and their organisations, government agencies), operating within a context (technology, markets and budgets, distribution of power in society), bound together by a shared ideology, and producing a web of rules: laws, settlements, standing orders and customs that govern the workplace.

    What good IR produces. Industrial peace, productivity, predictable costs, faster adoption of change and lower legal risk. Poor IR produces strikes and lockouts, litigation, low morale, absenteeism and, in the worst cases, violence.

    10.2Constitutional and legal foundations

    In short

    Indian labour law rests on Fundamental Rights, the Directive Principles of State Policy and the Concurrent List. India has not ratified the ILO conventions on freedom of association and collective bargaining.

    Recall

    • Art. 14, 16, 19(1)(c), 21, 23, 24
    • DPSPs: Art. 38, 39, 41, 42, 43, 43A
    • Concurrent List
    • ILO Conventions 87 and 98 not ratified

    Fundamental Rights (enforceable in courts)

    Article Content Relevance to labour
    14Equality before lawBasis for challenging arbitrary action by State employers
    16Equality of opportunity in public employmentPublic-sector recruitment and promotion
    19(1)(c)Freedom to form associations and unionsRight to form unions; the Supreme Court has held that this does not include a fundamental right to strike, which is regulated by statute
    21Right to life and personal libertyInterpreted to include livelihood and dignity
    23Prohibition of forced labourBonded labour, unpaid work
    24No employment of children below 14 in factories, mines or hazardous workChild labour laws

    Directive Principles of State Policy (guide legislation; not enforceable in courts)

    Article Content
    38Social order based on justice
    39Adequate livelihood; equal pay for equal work for men and women; protection of workers’ health
    41Right to work, education and public assistance in certain cases
    42Just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief
    43Living wage and decent standard of life
    43AParticipation of workers in the management of industries

    Labour is in the Concurrent List (entries on trade unions, industrial and labour disputes, social security, and welfare of labour), so both Parliament and state legislatures can make laws, and states frame rules under the central Codes. This is why compliance can differ between states.

    International standards. India is a founding member of the International Labour Organization and has ratified several core conventions (including those on forced labour, equal remuneration, discrimination and child labour), but not Conventions 87 (freedom of association) and 98 (right to organise and collective bargaining).

    10.3The Industrial Relations Code: scope and key definitions

    In short

    The IR Code merged three laws on unions, standing orders and industrial disputes. Its definitions of “industry” and “worker” decide who is covered.

    Recall

    • Merged: TU Act 1926, Standing Orders Act 1946, ID Act 1947
    • Industry: Rajappa triple test + exclusions
    • Worker: excludes managers and supervisors above ₹18,000
    • Industrial dispute includes individual termination

    The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 consolidated the Trade Unions Act, 1926, the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 and the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947. It covers four areas: organisation of workers, conditions of employment, settlement of disputes, and workforce restructuring (lay-off, retrenchment and closure).

    “Industry”

    Most IR provisions apply only to an industry. In Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (1978), a seven-judge bench of the Supreme Court gave a wide definition through the triple test: an activity is an industry if it is (1) a systematic activity, (2) organised by cooperation between employer and employees, (3) for the production or distribution of goods or services to satisfy human wants. Profit motive and capital investment are irrelevant. Where an organisation carries on several activities, the dominant nature of the activity decides.

    The IR Code codifies this broad approach but excludes: institutions owned or managed by organisations wholly or substantially engaged in charitable, social or philanthropic service; sovereign functions of the government (including defence, atomic energy and space); domestic service; and any other activity notified by the government.

    CorrectionThe compendium (p. 118) states that charitable organisations may qualify as industries. That was true under Rajappa, but the IR Code now excludes institutions wholly or substantially engaged in charitable, social or philanthropic service.

    “Worker”

    A worker is a person employed to do manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work for wages, and includes working journalists and sales promotion employees. It excludes persons employed mainly in a managerial or administrative capacity, and those in a supervisory capacity drawing wages above ₹18,000 a month (the limit was ₹10,000 under the Industrial Disputes Act). Whether a person is a worker depends on the main nature of their duties, not their title.

    “Industrial dispute”

    An industrial dispute is a dispute between employers and workers (or between workers) connected with employment, non-employment, terms of employment or conditions of labour. It includes a dispute between an individual worker and the employer over discharge, dismissal, retrenchment or termination, even if no union or group of workers supports it.

    10.4Trade unions

    In short

    Registered unions have legal status and immunities. The IR Code introduced union recognition: a sole negotiating union at 51% membership, otherwise a negotiating council of unions with at least 20%.

    Recall

    • Madras Labour Union 1918; AITUC 1920
    • BMS, INTUC, CITU, AITUC, HMS
    • Registration: 10% or 100 (min. 7)
    • Immunities
    • Recognition: 51% / 20%
    • Unfair labour practices

    Definition. A trade union is an association of workers (or employers) formed primarily to regulate relations between workers and employers and to protect and advance members’ interests.

    History. The Madras Labour Union, founded in 1918 with B. P. Wadia’s leadership, is regarded as India’s first organised trade union, and the All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) was founded in 1920. Most large union federations are affiliated with political parties: Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh (BMS), the largest, linked to the RSS; INTUC (Congress); CITU (CPI-M); AITUC (CPI); and Hind Mazdoor Sabha (HMS). Plants often have an internal union, a union affiliated with a federation, or several competing unions.

    Registration and status

    Element IR Code position
    Minimum membership for registrationAt least 10% of workers or 100 workers, whichever is less, with a minimum of 7 members
    Office-bearersAt least half must be workers actually engaged in the establishment or industry, with limited exceptions
    Legal statusA registered union is a body corporate that can hold property and sue or be sued
    ImmunitiesProtection from criminal conspiracy charges and civil suits for acts done in furtherance of a trade dispute, provided they are lawful

    Recognition of negotiating unions

    Before the Code, there was no central law requiring an employer to recognise a union for bargaining (some states, such as Maharashtra, had their own provisions). Employers often faced several rival unions with competing demands, or refused to deal with any. The IR Code introduced a recognition framework.

    Situation Result
    One union has 51% or more of the workers on the muster roll as membersIt is recognised as the sole negotiating union
    No union reaches 51%A negotiating council is formed, with representatives of unions that each have at least 20% membership

    Membership is verified under the rules. Why it matters: the employer has a clear bargaining counterpart, inter-union rivalry is reduced, and settlements are more likely to be accepted across the workforce.

    Unfair labour practices

    The Code lists unfair labour practices by both sides. Examples by employers: interfering with workers’ right to organise, threatening or victimising union members, establishing or supporting a company-controlled union, refusing to bargain in good faith with the recognised union, and mass transfers to break a union. Examples by workers or unions: coercing workers to join or not join a union, participating in illegal strikes, go-slow, gherao (confining managers) and violence. Unfair labour practices attract penalties.

    10.5Collective bargaining and settlements

    In short

    Collective bargaining is negotiation between employers and unions over terms of employment. It produces settlements, usually for three to four years, increasingly linked to productivity.

    Recall

    • Charter of demands
    • Distributive vs integrative bargaining
    • Bipartite settlement vs settlement in conciliation
    • Long-term settlements
    • Preparation data

    Definition. Collective bargaining is the process by which employers and workers’ representatives negotiate wages, working conditions and other terms of employment, and the rules that govern their relationship.

    Process

    Stage What happens
    Charter of demandsThe union submits written demands, typically wages, allowances, incentives, leave, welfare, manpower and job security, before the current settlement expires
    PreparationManagement analyses each demand: cost, regional and industry wage comparisons, productivity trends, ability to pay; prepares its own proposals
    NegotiationMeetings between the management team (plant head, HR, finance) and union representatives; positions narrow through trade-offs
    AgreementA written settlement signed by both parties, specifying terms and duration
    Implementation and administrationApplying the settlement, resolving interpretation disputes

    Types of bargaining (Walton and McKersie). Distributive bargaining divides a fixed amount: what one side gains, the other loses, typically on wage increases. Integrative bargaining seeks solutions that benefit both: for example, higher wages in exchange for multi-skilling, revised shift patterns or productivity improvements that reduce cost per unit.

    Settlements

    Type How it is made Who it binds
    Bipartite settlementAgreed directly between employer and unionThe parties to the agreement
    Settlement in conciliationAgreed during conciliation proceedings, signed before the conciliation officerAll workers in the establishment, including those not in the union

    Settlements in Indian manufacturing are usually long-term settlements for three to four years. Modern settlements are often productivity-linked: wage increases tied to agreed productivity norms, flexible deployment, multi-skilling or acceptance of automation, sometimes with job-security commitments.

    Preparing for a negotiation: a worked outline

    A cement plant union demands a 30% wage increase over three years. Management’s preparation would include: wages at comparable plants in the region and industry; inflation and consumer price trends; the plant’s cost per tonne, capacity utilisation and profitability (ability to pay); productivity trends and specific improvements management wants (shift flexibility, multi-skilling, manpower norms); the cost of each demand; a negotiation team and mandate; a communication plan for all employees; and contingency plans if talks fail. The likely outcome combines a lower wage increase with productivity commitments.

    10.6Standing orders

    In short

    Standing orders are the formal conditions of employment for workers: classification, hours, leave, misconduct, discipline and termination. Under the IR Code they apply to establishments with 300 or more workers.

    Recall

    • Threshold 300 (was 100)
    • Contents
    • Model Standing Orders, 2026
    • Schedules A, B, C

    Definition. Standing orders are written rules defining the conditions of employment of workers in an industrial establishment, so that both employer and workers know their rights and obligations.

    Contents typically include: classification of workers (permanent, probationer, badli or substitute, temporary, casual, apprentice, fixed-term); working hours, shifts and holidays; attendance and late arrival; leave; acts of misconduct; the disciplinary procedure, including suspension and punishments; termination and notice; and the grievance procedure.

    Aspect Standing Orders Act, 1946 IR Code, 2020
    ApplicabilityEstablishments with 100 or more workersEstablishments with 300 or more workers
    Model standing ordersCentral and state modelsModel Standing Orders, 2026: Schedule A (mines), B (manufacturing), C (services); employers may adopt them
    Fixed-term employmentRecognised only through amendmentsStatutory category

    Many establishments below 300 workers continue to maintain standing orders or equivalent service rules, because clear rules support fair discipline and reduce disputes.

    10.7Discipline and domestic enquiry

    In short

    Disciplinary action against workers must follow the standing orders and the principles of natural justice. A defective enquiry is the most common reason dismissals are overturned.

    Recall

    • Misconduct: minor and major
    • Charge-sheet, enquiry, findings, second show-cause, order
    • Natural justice
    • Subsistence allowance 50% → 75%
    • Proportionate punishment
    • Hot-stove rule

    Misconduct is conduct that breaches the standing orders, service rules or the employment relationship. Standing orders list acts of misconduct, typically including wilful insubordination, theft or fraud, wilful damage to property, habitual absence or lateness, assault or threatening behaviour, drunkenness on duty, sexual harassment, participating in an illegal strike, and habitual negligence.

    Steps in a domestic enquiry

    Step Purpose and key requirements
    1. Complaint or reportWritten record of the incident
    2. Preliminary inquiryFact-finding to decide whether there is a case
    3. Suspension pending enquiry (if needed)Used for serious misconduct. Subsistence allowance: 50% of wages for the first 90 days and 75% thereafter, if the delay is not caused by the worker
    4. Charge-sheet / show-cause noticeSpecific allegations with dates and details; reasonable time to reply
    5. Domestic enquiryHeld if the reply is unsatisfactory. An independent enquiry officer conducts it; a presenting officer presents management’s case; the worker may have a defence representative (often a co-worker or union representative); witnesses are examined and cross-examined; documents are shown to the worker; proceedings are recorded
    6. FindingsEnquiry officer’s report on whether charges are proved, with reasons
    7. Second show-causeWorker is given the findings and the proposed punishment and invited to respond (required by many standing orders and good practice)
    8. OrderPunishment proportionate to the misconduct and the worker’s record: warning, censure, fine, withholding of increment, suspension, demotion, discharge or dismissal

    Principles of natural justice. Audi alteram partem: the accused must be heard, know the evidence and have a fair chance to respond. Nemo judex in causa sua: no one may judge their own case, so the enquiry officer must be impartial and not a witness or complainant. Industrial tribunals can examine whether the enquiry was fair and whether the punishment was proportionate.

    Common defects: vague charges, an enquiry officer who was involved in the incident, denial of documents or of the chance to cross-examine, ignoring the worker’s evidence, and punishment out of proportion to the misconduct.

    Douglas McGregor’s “hot-stove rule” summarises good disciplinary practice: discipline should be immediate, given with advance warning, consistent and impersonal, like touching a hot stove.

    10.8Grievance handling and bipartite forums

    In short

    Grievances are handled first inside the organisation. The IR Code requires a Grievance Redressal Committee at 20 or more workers and a Works Committee at 100 or more.

    Recall

    • Grievance vs dispute
    • GRC: 20+, ≤10 members, 30 days
    • Works Committee: 100+, ≤20 members
    • Escalation to conciliation

    A grievance is an individual’s complaint about employment (pay, leave, transfer, treatment, working conditions). If unresolved it can become a dispute. Effective grievance handling resolves issues early and close to the workplace, which prevents escalation.

    Forum Applicability Composition and process
    Grievance Redressal Committee (GRC)Industrial establishments with 20 or more workersEqual representation of employer and workers, not more than 10 members, with women represented in proportion to their share of the workforce (central rules). Resolves individual grievances, generally within 30 days; an unresolved grievance can go to conciliation
    Works CommitteeEstablishments with 100 or more workersUp to 20 members, with worker representatives not fewer than employer representatives. Promotes cooperation and discusses common day-to-day issues; it does not replace collective bargaining

    Good practice in grievance handling: a written procedure with time limits at each level (supervisor, department head, GRC), listening and investigating before deciding, giving reasons, keeping records, and analysing grievances for patterns that indicate wider problems.

    10.9Dispute-resolution machinery

    In short

    Disputes move from internal forums to conciliation, then to voluntary arbitration or adjudication by Industrial Tribunals. The IR Code abolished Labour Courts and Boards of Conciliation.

    Recall

    • Conciliation officer
    • Voluntary arbitration
    • Industrial Tribunal (two members)
    • National Industrial Tribunal
    • Labour Courts abolished
    • Individual direct access
    Level Nature Role
    GRC and Works CommitteeInternal, bipartiteResolve individual grievances and day-to-day issues
    Conciliation officerGovernment officer, mediationHelps the parties reach a settlement; if conciliation fails, submits a failure report
    Voluntary arbitrationArbitrator chosen by both parties by written agreementBinding award
    Industrial TribunalAdjudication; two members, one judicial and one administrative, with provision for single-member benchesDecides disputes referred to it or brought by workers; its award is binding
    National Industrial TribunalConstituted by the Central GovernmentDisputes of national importance or affecting establishments in more than one state

    Changes under the IR Code. Labour Courts, Boards of Conciliation and Courts of Inquiry were abolished; adjudication is now by Industrial Tribunals. An individual worker with a dispute over discharge, dismissal, retrenchment or termination can apply directly to the Tribunal after the conciliation period has passed without settlement, rather than waiting for the government to refer the dispute, subject to a limitation period.

    Who approaches whom. An individual grievance goes first to the GRC. Day-to-day cooperation issues go to the Works Committee. A collective dispute goes to the conciliation officer. A failed conciliation leads to arbitration (if both agree) or the Industrial Tribunal. Disputes of national scope go to the National Industrial Tribunal.

    10.10Strikes and lockouts

    In short

    Strikes and lockouts are lawful only if notice and timing conditions are met. The IR Code extended notice requirements to all industrial establishments and treats mass casual leave as a strike.

    Recall

    • Definitions; forms of strike
    • Notice within 60 days; not within 14 days
    • Not during conciliation + 7 days; tribunal/arbitration + 60 days
    • Mass casual leave (50%+) = strike
    • Illegal strike: no wages
    • Managing a strike

    Definitions. A strike is a cessation of work by a body of workers acting in combination, or a concerted refusal to work. Under the IR Code it includes concerted casual leave by 50% or more of the workers on a given day. A lockout is the temporary closure of a place of employment, suspension of work or refusal by an employer to continue employing workers, used as pressure in a dispute.

    Forms of strike. General strike; sit-down or stay-in strike (workers occupy the premises without working); tool-down or pen-down strike; go-slow (deliberately reducing output while remaining at work, generally treated as misconduct); work-to-rule (following every rule exactly to slow work); token strike (short, symbolic); sympathetic strike (supporting other workers); hunger strike. A gherao (physically confining managers) is unlawful.

    Conditions for a lawful strike or lockout (all industrial establishments)

    Condition Rule
    NoticeMust be given within 60 days before the strike or lockout
    Waiting periodNo strike or lockout within 14 days of giving notice, or before the date specified in the notice
    During conciliationProhibited while conciliation is pending and for 7 days after it ends
    During adjudication or arbitrationProhibited while proceedings are pending before a Tribunal or arbitrator and for 60 days after they conclude
    During a settlement or awardProhibited on matters covered by an operative settlement or award

    Under the Industrial Disputes Act, notice was required mainly in public utility services, so sudden (“flash”) strikes were lawful elsewhere. The Code’s extension to all establishments was welcomed by employers for predictability and criticised by unions as a restriction on protest.

    Consequences of an illegal strike: no wages for the strike period (“no work, no pay”), penalties for participants and instigators, and possible disciplinary action. Wages for a legal strike may be awarded only if the strike is found both legal and justified.

    Managing a strike: the HR role

    Ensure safety and security of people and plant; maintain critical operations lawfully using non-striking staff; open immediate dialogue with union leaders; record facts; inform the conciliation officer and, only where needed, the police; communicate clearly with all employees about the legal position and wages; avoid victimisation; and, after resolution, address the underlying grievances.

    10.11Lay-off, retrenchment and closure

    In short

    Lay-off is temporary inability to provide work; retrenchment is termination for reasons other than punishment; closure is permanent shutdown. Government permission is needed at 300 or more workers. A reskilling-fund payment is added to retrenchment compensation.

    Recall

    • Definitions and exclusions
    • Lay-off: 50% of basic + DA
    • Retrenchment: 1 month notice, 15 days per year, LIFO
    • 300+: permission, 3 months’ notice
    • Reskilling fund: 15 days’ wages
    • Re-employment preference (1 year)

    Definitions

    Lay-off is the failure, refusal or inability of an employer to give employment to a worker on the muster roll because of shortage of coal, power or raw materials, accumulation of stocks, breakdown of machinery, natural calamity or similar reasons. The employment relationship continues.

    Retrenchment is termination of a worker’s service by the employer for any reason other than punishment through disciplinary action. It does not include: voluntary retirement; superannuation; non-renewal of a fixed-term contract on expiry; and termination on grounds of continued ill-health.

    Closure is the permanent closing down of a place of employment or part of it.

    Comparison

    Lay-off Retrenchment Closure
    NatureTemporaryPermanent, for individualsPermanent, for the establishment or part
    Employment relationshipContinuesEnds for retrenched workersEnds for all affected workers
    Compensation50% of basic + DA for days laid off (generally limited to 45 days in 12 months unless otherwise agreed)15 days’ average pay for each completed year of continuous service (or part over six months)Same as retrenchment
    Conditions (establishments below 300 workers)One month’s notice or pay in lieu; notice to the appropriate government; last in, first out within a category unless reasons are recorded60 days’ notice to the government for establishments with 50 or more workers
    Establishments with 300 or more workersPrior government permissionPrior permission; three months’ notice or pay in lieuPrior permission, applied for at least 90 days before
    Reskilling fundNoYesAs provided for retrenched workers

    The 300-worker threshold for permission was 100 under the Industrial Disputes Act; states may raise it further.

    Worker Re-skilling Fund. For each retrenched worker, the employer pays an amount equal to 15 days’ last drawn wages. Under the central rules (May 2026) the employer transfers it within 10 days of retrenchment to a designated government account, and it is credited to the worker within 45 days. It is in addition to retrenchment compensation.

    Re-employment. Retrenched workers have preference for re-employment for one year after retrenchment. Vacancies must be displayed at least 15 days before filling, and eligible workers informed.

    Worked example. A worker with 8 years and 7 months of continuous service and average pay of ₹26,000 a month is retrenched. Completed years count as 9 (the part year exceeds six months). Retrenchment compensation = 15 ÷ 26 × 26,000 × 9 = ₹1,35,000. Reskilling fund = 15 ÷ 26 × 26,000 = ₹15,000. Plus one month’s notice pay of ₹26,000 if notice is not given.

    Voluntary retirement schemes (VRS) are the most common way Indian employers reduce workforces with less conflict: an offer of enhanced compensation for employees who choose to leave, usually negotiated with the union. A VRS exit is not retrenchment.

    10.12Fixed-term employment

    In short

    Fixed-term employment is direct employment for a defined period, with the same pay and benefits as permanent workers and gratuity after one year.

    Recall

    • Written contract, direct employment
    • Parity in hours, wages, benefits
    • Gratuity after 1 year, pro rata
    • Non-renewal ≠ retrenchment

    Definition. Fixed-term employment (FTE) is the engagement of a worker directly by the employer, under a written contract, for a fixed period.

    Conditions under the IR Code: hours of work, wages, allowances and other benefits must be no less favourable than those of permanent workers doing the same or similar work; fixed-term workers are eligible for statutory benefits in proportion to their service; and they are eligible for gratuity after one year of service. Non-renewal on expiry is not retrenchment.

    Why it matters. FTE gives employers flexibility for seasonal demand, projects and ramp-ups without using contractors, while giving workers parity of pay and benefits. Unions are concerned that it can replace permanent jobs with successive short contracts; repeated renewals for permanent, perennial work carry legal and IR risk.

    10.13Workers’ participation in management

    In short

    Article 43A directs the State to secure workers’ participation in management. In practice it takes the form of works committees, joint councils, worker directors and consultation.

    Recall

    • Art. 43A
    • Levels: information, consultation, joint decision
    • Works committees, joint management councils
    • Worker directors in some PSUs

    Workers’ participation in management (WPM) ranges from information sharing, through consultation, to joint decision-making. Indian forms include works committees (statutory), joint management councils and shop councils (introduced through government schemes from the 1950s and 1970s), worker representatives on boards in some public-sector undertakings, and joint committees on safety, productivity and canteens. WPM has had limited success where unions are politically divided or management treats forums as formalities; it works where forums have real decisions to influence, such as safety or shift design.

    10.14Lessons from Indian IR cases

    In short

    Major Indian disputes show recurring causes: blocked union recognition, pay gaps between permanent and contract workers, wage non-payment and weak grievance channels.

    Recall

    • Maruti Manesar 2012
    • Honda Manesar 2005
    • Wistron Kolar 2020
    • Samsung Sriperumbudur 2024
    • Tata Steel: industrial harmony
    Case What happened Lessons for HR
    Maruti Suzuki, Manesar (2012)After months of tension over registration of a new union and the large share of contract workers on lower pay, violence broke out in July 2012; HR manager Awanish Kumar Dev was killed and part of the plant burned. In 2017 a court convicted 31 workers, 13 of them to life imprisonmentPay disparity between contract and permanent workers doing similar work, obstruction of union recognition and weak grievance channels create explosive conditions; early warning signs must be acted on
    Honda Motorcycle and Scooter, Manesar (2005)Disputes over union formation and dismissals led to violent clashes between workers and policeRespecting the right to organise prevents escalation
    Wistron, Kolar (December 2020)Workers at an iPhone assembly plant, many engaged through contractors, rioted over unpaid and short-paid wages and overtime; Apple placed the supplier on probationPrincipal employers are accountable for contractors’ wage compliance; payroll accuracy is an IR issue
    Samsung, Sriperumbudur (September–October 2024)A strike lasting over a month, led by a union affiliated with CITU, over wages and union recognition; ended after mediation by the Tamil Nadu government; the registration question went to the Madras High CourtUnion recognition is the central IR issue in many newer multinational plants
    Tata SteelLong record of industrial harmony built on joint consultation with the Tata Workers’ Union and extensive welfare; large workforce reduction in the 1990s and 2000s achieved mainly through voluntary separation schemesTrust and joint mechanisms are long-term assets that make difficult change possible

    Early warning signs of IR trouble: rising grievances and absenteeism, go-slow or refusal of overtime, sudden union activity or rival unions, contract workers’ complaints about pay, supervisors’ complaints about indiscipline, and leaflets or social-media campaigns. HR should track these and respond before positions harden.

    10.15Old law and the IR Code: summary

    In short

    The Code increased flexibility for employers on thresholds, fixed-term employment and strike notice, and added recognition, a reskilling fund and faster adjudication for workers.

    10.16Interview questions

    In short

    IR questions test legal accuracy and practical judgement. Give the rule, then show how you would handle the situation.

    What is the difference between lay-off, retrenchment and closure?

    Lay-off is a temporary inability to provide work, for reasons such as power or raw-material shortage, and the employment relationship continues; compensation is 50% of basic and DA. Retrenchment is permanent termination for reasons other than disciplinary punishment, requiring a month’s notice, 15 days’ pay per completed year, last-in-first-out and a reskilling-fund payment. Closure is the permanent shutdown of the establishment. Establishments with 300 or more workers need prior government permission for all three.

    How is a union recognised under the IR Code?

    A union with 51% or more of the workers on the muster roll as members becomes the sole negotiating union. If none reaches 51%, a negotiating council is formed of unions that each have at least 20%. Membership is verified under the rules. It gives the employer one bargaining counterpart and reduces inter-union rivalry.

    Walk me through a domestic enquiry.

    After a preliminary inquiry, the worker receives a charge-sheet with specific allegations and time to reply, and may be suspended on subsistence allowance. An impartial enquiry officer holds the enquiry, with a presenting officer for management and a defence representative for the worker; witnesses are examined and cross-examined and documents disclosed. The officer records findings with reasons, the worker gets a second show-cause notice with the proposed punishment, and the final order must be proportionate. Natural justice throughout is essential, because defective enquiries are the main reason dismissals are set aside.

    Is the IR Code pro-employer or pro-worker?

    It increases employer flexibility through the 300-worker threshold, fixed-term employment and strike notice in all establishments. It also gives workers union recognition, a reskilling fund, a wider definition of worker and faster adjudication. On balance it trades flexibility for clearer and faster processes; its real effect depends on state rules and enforcement.

    The union calls a flash strike without notice. What do you do?

    Secure people and the plant, keep essential operations running lawfully, and open dialogue with union leaders immediately. Record the facts, inform the conciliation officer, and communicate to all employees that the strike is illegal for lack of notice and that wages will not be paid for the period. Avoid victimisation, and once work resumes, deal with the grievance that caused the strike.

    Automation will make 120 roles surplus in a 900-worker plant. How would you handle it?

    Plan early with the union: redeploy and retrain first, use natural attrition and a voluntary retirement scheme with fair terms, and link the change to the wage settlement. If retrenchment is still needed, the plant (300+ workers) needs prior government permission, three months’ notice, 15 days’ pay per year, the reskilling-fund payment, last-in-first-out and re-employment preference.
