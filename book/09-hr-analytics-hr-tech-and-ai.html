    HR Analytics, HR Technology and AI

    Chapter 9 · HR Analytics, HR Technology and AI

    What this chapter covers. The types and maturity of HR analytics; a structured analytics process; data foundations; the statistical ideas every HR professional needs; the main HR analyses (attrition, pay equity, selection, absenteeism, engagement, networks); metrics and dashboards; well-known cases; HR technology systems and their implementation; artificial intelligence in HR and its governance; and India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 as it applies to employee data.

    Why it matters for interviews. Employers increasingly expect HR graduates to use data. Expect questions such as “How would you use analytics to reduce attrition?”, “What could go wrong with an AI model?” and “Do we need consent to process employee data?”.

    9.1What HR analytics is

    In short

    HR analytics uses workforce data and statistical methods to improve people decisions. It progresses from describing what happened to recommending what to do.

    Recall

    • Metrics vs analytics vs people analytics
    • Descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, prescriptive
    • Bersin’s four levels
    • Why it matters

    Definitions. HR metrics are measures of HR activity and outcomes, such as attrition rate or time-to-fill. HR analytics (or people analytics) is the systematic use of workforce data, statistical analysis and increasingly machine learning to understand people-related outcomes and improve decisions. The term “people analytics” is often used for the broader, business-focused practice that combines HR data with operational and financial data.

    Types of analytics

    Type Question HR example Typical methods
    DescriptiveWhat happened?Attrition by department last quarterReports, dashboards
    DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Attrition is concentrated in three managers’ teams and among employees paid below 85% of their range midpointSegmentation, correlation, driver analysis, text analysis of exit comments
    PredictiveWhat is likely to happen?Probability that each employee will leave within six monthsLogistic regression, survival analysis, machine learning
    PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Which retention action gives the best return for which groupOptimisation, simulation, experiments

    Maturity. Josh Bersin’s widely used model describes four levels: (1) operational reporting (accurate basic reports), (2) advanced reporting (benchmarks, dashboards, trends), (3) strategic analytics (segmentation, causal analysis, linking people data to business outcomes) and (4) predictive analytics (forecasting and scenario modelling). Most organisations operate at levels 1 and 2; few sustain level 4.

    Why it matters. Analytics improves the quality of people decisions, gives HR evidence in business discussions, tests assumptions (managers’ beliefs about why people leave are often wrong), and targets scarce resources, such as retention budgets, where they have most effect.

    9.2The analytics process

    In short

    Start with a business question and a decision, not with data. Follow a disciplined process from question to measured action.

    Recall

    • Seven steps
    • Business question first
    • Hypotheses
    • LAMP (Boudreau & Ramstad)
    1. Define the business question and the decision it will inform. “Which plant operators are likely to leave in the next six months, and what would keep them?” is better than “build an attrition dashboard”.
    2. Form hypotheses from managers, research and experience: pay below market, shift patterns, commute distance, supervisor behaviour, lack of progression.
    3. Gather and prepare data from the HRIS, payroll, attendance, performance, engagement surveys, exit data and external sources; clean, define and join it.
    4. Analyse, starting with simple segmentation and visualisation before modelling.
    5. Interpret with business context: is the effect large enough to matter, and is it causal?
    6. Act: a small number of actions with owners and timelines.
    7. Measure the effect, ideally by comparing a pilot group with a similar group that did not receive the intervention.

    The LAMP framework (John Boudreau and Peter Ramstad) holds that measurement improves decisions only when four elements are present: Logic (a clear model of how people factors drive business outcomes), Analytics (sound methods), Measures (reliable data) and Process (communication and decision processes that turn insight into action).

    9.3Data foundations

    In short

    Analytics depends on accurate, consistently defined and lawfully used data. Most of the effort in real projects goes into data preparation.

    Recall

    • Data sources
    • Common definitions
    • Quality problems
    • Joining data
    • Privacy by design
    Source Typical data
    HRIS / HRMSEmployee master data, job, grade, location, manager, tenure, movements
    PayrollPay, allowances, increments, bonuses
    Time and attendanceAttendance, leave, overtime, shifts
    Performance and talentRatings, goals, potential, succession status
    Recruitment (ATS)Source, time-to-hire, assessment scores
    Learning (LMS)Courses, completions, assessments
    Engagement and surveysSurvey scores, comments, eNPS
    Exit dataExit reasons, interviews
    Business systemsProductivity, sales, quality, safety, customer data

    Common data problems: inconsistent definitions (does “attrition” include transfers and contract staff?), missing or outdated records, inconsistent job titles (one reason job architecture matters, Chapter 6), different employee identifiers across systems, and small groups that make results unstable or identify individuals.

    Privacy by design: use only the data needed for the purpose, aggregate where possible, restrict access, and follow the DPDP Act (Section 9.10).

    9.4Statistical foundations

    In short

    A few statistical ideas prevent most analytical mistakes: distributions, correlation versus causation, regression, significance and sample size, and controlled comparison.

    Recall

    • Mean vs median
    • Correlation ≠ causation
    • Linear vs logistic regression
    • Multicollinearity (VIF)
    • Statistical vs practical significance
    • Confounding; Simpson’s paradox
    • Experiments and control groups
    Concept Meaning HR relevance
    Mean and medianAverage; middle valuePay data is skewed by high earners, so medians are more representative
    Distribution and varianceSpread of valuesRating distributions; pay dispersion within grades
    CorrelationStrength of linear association between two variables, from −1 to +1Engagement and productivity move together, but correlation alone does not show which causes which
    CausationOne variable changes anotherEstablished through experiments or careful designs that control other factors
    Linear regressionModels a continuous outcome (such as pay) as a function of several variablesPay-equity analysis
    Logistic regressionModels the probability of a yes/no outcome (such as leaving)Attrition risk models
    Statistical significanceWhether an observed effect is unlikely to be due to chance (often p < 0.05)Small HR samples often produce unstable results
    Practical significanceWhether the effect is large enough to matterA statistically significant 0.5% pay difference may not justify action, while a 10% difference would
    ConfoundingA third variable affects both the factor and the outcomeRemote workers may appear to leave more because they are newer, not because they are remote
    Simpson’s paradoxA trend in combined data reverses within subgroupsWomen may appear to be promoted less overall but at equal or higher rates within every function, because they are concentrated in functions with fewer promotions
    MulticollinearityPredictors that are highly correlated with each other, making individual effects unreliable; checked with the variance inflation factor (VIF)Age and tenure in an attrition model
    ExperimentsRandomly assigning a change to one group and comparing it with a control groupTesting a new onboarding programme in some locations first

    9.5Key HR analyses

    In short

    The most common analyses are attrition drivers and risk, pay equity, selection validity, absenteeism, engagement–performance links and organisational networks.

    Recall

    • Attrition: logistic regression, survival analysis
    • Pay equity: regression on log pay
    • Selection validity
    • Bradford factor
    • Engagement–performance
    • ONA
    • Ethical limits

    Attrition analysis

    Descriptive and diagnostic: segment attrition by role, tenure, manager, location, pay position, performance and engagement; compare leavers with stayers.

    Predictive: a logistic regression estimates each employee’s probability of leaving from variables such as tenure, compa-ratio, time since last promotion, rating, manager change, commute distance, overtime and engagement score. The coefficients show which factors increase risk. Survival analysis models when people leave, which is useful for early attrition. Machine-learning models can improve prediction but are harder to explain.

    Interpretation example. A model shows that operators whose pay is below 90% of the range midpoint and who have had no skill progression in two years have roughly twice the attrition risk of others, holding other factors constant. The actions follow directly: a targeted pay correction and a skills-progression scheme, tested first in one plant.

    Ethical limit: risk scores should guide support (career conversations, pay review), never exclusion from opportunities, and individuals’ scores should not be shown to managers without safeguards.

    Pay-equity analysis

    Regress the logarithm of pay on legitimate factors (grade, job family, experience, performance rating, location) plus a gender indicator. The gender coefficient estimates the adjusted pay gap: the difference not explained by the legitimate factors. A large raw gap with a small adjusted gap points to representation problems (hiring and promotion) rather than pay setting (Chapter 2, Section 2.24).

    Other analyses

    Analysis Method Use and caution
    Selection validityCorrelate assessment and interview scores with later performance and retentionShows which selection methods work; only hired candidates have outcomes, which understates validity
    AbsenteeismPatterns by day, shift, supervisor and season; the Bradford factor = S² × D, where S is the number of spells and D the total days absentHighlights frequent short absences: ten one-day absences score 10² × 10 = 1,000, while one ten-day absence scores 1 × 10 = 10. Use for conversations, not automatic penalties, to avoid penalising genuine illness or disability
    Engagement and performanceLink team engagement scores to productivity, quality, safety and customer measuresCausality runs both ways
    Organisational network analysis (ONA)Map collaboration from surveys or communication metadataIdentifies isolated teams and key connectors; needs a clear purpose, aggregation and transparency because of privacy
    Workforce planningMarkov and scenario models (Chapter 6)Assumptions drive results

    In short

    A good dashboard shows a few decision-relevant metrics, with trends, targets and segments, for a specific audience.

    Core metrics

    Area Metric Formula or definition
    WorkforceHeadcount and FTECount at a date; FTE adjusts for part-time staff

    Dashboard design

    • Start from decisions. Each metric should prompt a question or action for its audience.
    • Few metrics: 6–10 for a leadership dashboard, with drill-downs for detail.
    • Context: show trends over time, targets or benchmarks, and segments.
    • Consistency: shared definitions across the organisation.
    • Readable design: clear labels, minimal decoration, colour used only to flag exceptions.
    Audience Typical content
    Board and CEOHeadcount and cost, revenue per employee, regretted attrition, bench strength for critical roles, engagement, safety (for plants), diversity
    Business leadersOpen positions and time-to-fill, attrition by team, performance distribution, overtime, absenteeism
    HR centres of excellenceRecruitment funnel, compa-ratio distributions, learning reach and application

    Limitations: dashboards depend on data quality and integration, can be costly to build, and are often not used if they do not answer leaders’ questions. They report compliance status but do not ensure compliance.

    9.7Well-known cases

    In short

    Documented cases show analytics changing beliefs, cutting costs and improving fairness, and also the need to treat vendor claims carefully.

    Recall

    • Google: four interviews
    • IBM attrition prediction (2019)
    • Salesforce pay audits (from 2015)
    Case What was done Point it illustrates
    Google Project Oxygen (2008–09)Google’s people analytics team tested whether managers matter, using performance reviews, feedback surveys and award nominations. They found that managers did affect team performance and retention, and identified eight (later ten) behaviours of effective managers, such as being a good coach, empowering the team without micromanaging and expressing interest in team members’ success.     Google’s interview researchAnalysis of hiring data found that four interviews predicted hiring decisions about as accurately as more, so Google reduced the number of interviews and dropped brain-teaser questions that did not predict performanceAnalytics reduces cost and improves candidate experience
    IBMIBM’s then chief executive said in 2019 that the company’s AI could predict which employees were about to leave with about 95% accuracy and had saved about US$300 million in retention costsPrediction has value only when paired with action; the figures are IBM’s own
    SalesforceAnnual pay audits since 2015, adjusting pay for employees where unexplained gaps by gender or ethnicity were foundAnalytics as a tool for fairness

    9.8HR technology

    In short

    HR technology runs from core records (HRIS) to process suites (HRMS) and talent platforms (HCM). Implementations succeed when processes are redesigned and adoption is managed.

    Recall

    • HRIS, HRMS, HCM
    • ATS, LMS/LXP, payroll, engagement tools
    • ESS and MSS
    • Indian vendors
    • Implementation steps
    • Failure reasons

    Systems

    System Scope Examples
    HRIS (human resource information system)Core employee data: records, organisation structure, job and pay history, attendance and leaveCore modules of most suites
    HRMS (human resource management system)HRIS plus processes: recruitment, performance, learning, payroll integrationDarwinbox, Keka, Zoho People
    HCM (human capital management)HRMS plus strategic talent management: succession, workforce planning, analyticsWorkday HCM, SAP SuccessFactors, Oracle HCM
    ATSRecruitment workflowGreenhouse, Lever, Darwinbox Recruit
    LMS and LXPLearning administration and experience (Chapter 4)Cornerstone, Degreed
    Engagement and listening platformsSurveys, pulses, recognitionWorkday Peakon, inFeedo Amber, Vantage Circle
    ESS and MSSEmployee and manager self-serviceLeave, payslips, approvals, profile updates on mobile
    HR chatbots and assistantsAnswer queries, guide transactionsMcDonald’s “Olivia” for hourly hiring; IBM’s AI assistant for HR queries

    The labels HRIS, HRMS and HCM overlap in vendors’ marketing; the useful distinction is between core records, process automation and strategic talent management.

    India contextIndian HR technology has grown rapidly. Darwinbox (Hyderabad), a unicorn since 2022, serves large Indian groups and has expanded across Asia; Keka, greytHR, Zoho People, PeopleStrong and ZingHR serve many mid-sized firms. Indian payroll is complex (state professional tax and labour welfare funds, PF and ESI, two tax regimes, the Labour Codes’ wage definition), which favours vendors built for India in the mid-market. Large groups and multinationals often run SAP SuccessFactors or Workday globally with a local payroll partner.

    Implementation

    1. Select: define requirements, shortlist vendors, see demonstrations using your own scenarios, check references, compare total cost of ownership.
    2. Plan: steering committee, project team, timeline, change-management plan.
    3. Design: map and redesign processes, define the data model, plan integrations (payroll, finance, attendance devices, single sign-on).
    4. Configure and test: data migration and cleansing; user acceptance testing with real users.
    5. Train and communicate: managers first, since they approve most transactions.
    6. Go live and sustain: a support period after go-live, adoption tracking, continuous improvement and governance.

    Why implementations fail: migrating dirty data unchanged; automating poor processes instead of redesigning them; excessive customisation that makes upgrades difficult; weak manager adoption; no clear owner after go-live; and underestimating integration with payroll and attendance.

    9.9Artificial intelligence in HR

    In short

    AI can make HR faster and more personalised, but it can also scale bias, invade privacy and make decisions people cannot explain. Governance keeps humans accountable.

    Recall

    • Predictive ML vs generative AI vs agents
    • Uses across the lifecycle
    • Risks: bias, opacity, hallucination, privacy, surveillance
    • Governance principles
    • EU AI Act; NYC LL144; India AI Governance Guidelines

    Types. Predictive machine learning estimates outcomes from data (attrition risk, candidate match). Generative AI produces text and other content (job descriptions, policies, summaries, learning content, answers to employee questions). AI agents carry out multi-step tasks such as scheduling interviews or processing routine requests.

    Lifecycle stage Uses
    RecruitmentCV screening and matching, chatbots, interview scheduling, JD drafting (Chapter 1)
    Onboarding and serviceHR assistants answering policy questions, guided transactions
    LearningPersonalised recommendations, AI tutors, content creation
    Performance and engagementFeedback summaries, sentiment analysis of comments, rating-pattern checks
    Workforce planningSkills inference, demand forecasting, attrition prediction

    Risks.

    Risk Example Mitigation
    BiasAmazon’s experimental CV-screening tool learned to penalise CVs mentioning “women’s” (abandoned in 2018)Test outcomes by group before and after deployment; remove proxies for protected characteristics
    OpacityCandidates or employees cannot understand why a decision was madePrefer explainable models for decisions about people; document logic
    Inaccuracy (hallucination)A generative AI assistant gives a wrong answer on leave policyGround answers in approved policy documents; human review for sensitive queries
    Privacy and confidentialityEmployee data entered into public AI toolsApproved enterprise tools, data-protection agreements, DPDP compliance
    Surveillance and trustMonitoring keystrokes or sentiment of individualsPurpose limitation, aggregation, transparency

    Governance principles: keep a human accountable for decisions affecting people; test for bias before and during use; explain to employees and candidates how AI is used; minimise data; assess vendors; and maintain an inventory of AI uses.

    Regulation. The EU AI Act (in force since 2024) classifies AI used in recruitment and employee management as high-risk, with obligations on risk management, data quality, transparency and human oversight phasing in from 2026–27. New York City’s Local Law 144 (2023) requires independent bias audits of automated employment decision tools. India has no AI-specific law; MeitY’s India AI Governance Guidelines (November 2025) set out principles, and the DPDP Act is the main legal constraint on HR uses of personal data.

    9.10The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023

    In short

    The DPDP Act governs digital personal data in India. Employers may process employee data for employment purposes without consent as a legitimate use, but must keep it secure, limited to purpose, and report breaches.

    Recall

    • Data principal, fiduciary, processor
    • Consent vs legitimate use (s.7)
    • Notice
    • Duties and rights
    • Breach: 72-hour report (Rules)
    • SDF and DPO
    • Penalties up to ₹250 crore
    • Rules: 13 Nov 2025; phased to ~May 2027

    Key concepts

    Concept Meaning for HR
    Data principalThe person the data is about: employee, candidate, former employee
    Data fiduciaryThe organisation deciding why and how data is processed: the employer
    Data processorAn entity processing data on the fiduciary’s behalf: payroll provider, HRMS vendor, background-verification agency
    ScopeDigital personal data, and personal data collected in non-digital form and later digitised

    Lawful grounds

    Personal data may be processed either with consent (free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given after a notice) or for a legitimate use listed in section 7. One legitimate use is processing for the purposes of employment, or to safeguard the employer from loss or liability, for example to prevent corporate espionage, maintain confidentiality of trade secrets or provide benefits to employees. Processing payroll, attendance and performance data therefore does not require separate consent. Uses outside employment purposes, such as using employees’ photographs in marketing, require consent.

    Obligations of the employer as data fiduciary

    • Give a clear notice of what data is collected and why, where consent is the basis.
    • Keep data accurate and complete where it is used for decisions.
    • Implement reasonable security safeguards.
    • Erase data when the purpose is served and retention is no longer required by law.
    • Establish grievance redressal for data principals.
    • Bind processors by contract; the fiduciary remains responsible for their processing.
    • Report breaches: inform affected individuals and the Data Protection Board without delay, and submit a detailed report to the Board within 72 hours of becoming aware (DPDP Rules, 2025).

    Rights of data principals: access to information about processing, correction and erasure, grievance redressal, and nomination of a person to exercise rights on their behalf.

    Significant Data Fiduciaries (notified by the government on the basis of volume and sensitivity of data and risk) must appoint a Data Protection Officer based in India, appoint independent auditors and conduct data protection impact assessments.

    Cross-border transfers are permitted except to countries the government restricts by notification.

    Penalties are financial and set by the Data Protection Board: up to ₹250 crore for failing to take reasonable security safeguards, up to ₹200 crore for failing to notify a breach, with other amounts for other breaches.

    Timeline. The DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025. Provisions establishing the Data Protection Board took effect immediately; consent-manager provisions follow after 12 months; most substantive obligations, including notice, security safeguards and breach reporting, apply after 18 months (around May 2027).

    DPDP and GDPR

    Aspect DPDP Act (India) GDPR (European Union)
    Data coveredDigital personal data, and data digitised laterDigital and structured paper records
    Employment processingLegitimate use for employment purposes; consent not requiredLawful bases such as contract and legitimate interests; consent is generally considered unsuitable in employment because of the power imbalance
    Breach notificationDetailed report to the Board within 72 hours (Rules)Notify the supervisory authority within 72 hours
    Data protection officerRequired for Significant Data FiduciariesRequired for large-scale processing of sensitive data and certain other cases
    Cross-border transfersPermitted except to restricted countriesPermitted to countries with adequate protection or with safeguards
    PenaltiesFixed maximums up to ₹250 crore per instanceUp to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover

    CorrectionThe compendium’s comparison table (pp. 111–112) states that under DPDP “HR must get written or digital consent for storing employee details”. That is incorrect: employment purposes are a legitimate use without consent. It also states there is no breach-reporting deadline; the 2025 Rules require a detailed report within 72 hours. And it describes cross-border transfer rules as unclear, whereas the Act permits transfers except to restricted countries.

    HR implications

    • Map employee and candidate data: what is held, where, why, for how long and who can access it.
    • Update notices and contracts; obtain consent for non-employment uses and for background verification where required.
    • Review vendor contracts (payroll, HRMS, background verification, benefits brokers).
    • Define retention and deletion schedules, for example for rejected candidates.
    • Set rules for employee monitoring based on purpose and proportionality.
    • Prepare a breach response plan and train HR staff in data handling.

    Principles for employee monitoring (Gartner): purpose (state why data is collected), minimisation (collect the least needed), fairness (use data to support employees, not only to police them) and awareness (tell employees what is collected and how it is used).

    9.11Interview questions

    In short

    Show that you start from a business question, understand the limits of data and models, and know the legal position.

    How would you use analytics to reduce attrition?

    Define the question and decision, segment attrition by role, tenure, manager and pay position, and test hypotheses with data from the HRIS, payroll, ratings, surveys and exit interviews. Build a logistic regression to identify drivers and risk, then design targeted actions such as pay corrections, manager coaching or career moves, pilot them, and measure regretted attrition against a comparison group. Use risk scores to offer support, never to exclude people.

    What is the difference between HRIS, HRMS and HCM?

    An HRIS manages core employee records, an HRMS adds HR processes such as recruitment, performance and learning, and an HCM suite adds strategic talent management such as succession, workforce planning and analytics. The labels overlap in vendor marketing.

    Do we need employees’ consent to process payroll data under the DPDP Act?

    No. Processing for employment purposes is a legitimate use under section 7. The employer must still keep the data secure and accurate, use it only for that purpose, delete it when no longer needed, bind vendors by contract and report breaches. Consent is needed for uses outside employment purposes.

    What could go wrong with an AI model that predicts attrition?

    It may learn bias from historical data or use proxies for protected characteristics, such as leave patterns that correlate with gender. Managers may stop investing in people flagged as likely to leave, making the prediction self-fulfilling. Sensitive data may be exposed, and trust may suffer if employees discover it. Governance, bias testing, transparency and human decisions reduce these risks.

    Will AI replace HR professionals?

    It will replace many HR tasks, such as query handling, screening, drafting and reporting, but not the work that depends on judgement and trust: employee relations, negotiation, change leadership, ethics and coaching managers. HR roles will shift towards analytics, design and advisory work, and HR will lead the workforce side of AI adoption for the whole organisation.
