    Engagement, Well-being, Employee Experience and EVP

    Chapter 5 · Engagement, Well-being, Employee Experience and EVP

    What this chapter covers. The concepts of engagement, satisfaction, employee experience and well-being and how they differ; the main engagement models; what drives engagement; how engagement is measured and turned into action; attrition and retention; how engagement differs between plant, corporate, frontline and hybrid workforces; well-being and burnout, including the Indian legal and policy context; employee experience design; and the employee value proposition (EVP) and its relationship to employer brand.

    Why it matters for interviews. Attrition, engagement surveys and EVP are among the most common scenario questions.

    5.1Core concepts

    In short

    Engagement is active commitment that shows up as discretionary effort. Satisfaction is contentment. Employee experience is the whole journey that produces both. Well-being is the health that sustains them.

    Recall

    • Engagement (Kahn; Schaufeli)
    • Satisfaction
    • Organisational commitment
    • Employee experience (EX)
    • Well-being
    • Why engagement matters

    Definitions

    Employee engagement is a positive, active state of commitment to one’s work and organisation that shows in discretionary effort: doing more than the role strictly requires. Two academic definitions are widely cited. William Kahn (1990) described personal engagement as people bringing themselves physically, cognitively and emotionally into their work roles. Wilmar Schaufeli and colleagues defined work engagement as a state characterised by vigour (energy and persistence), dedication (enthusiasm and pride) and absorption (being engrossed in the work), measured by the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale.

    Job satisfaction is how content an employee is with their job and its conditions. It is passive: a satisfied employee is not necessarily willing to give extra effort.

    Organisational commitment is the employee’s attachment to the organisation. Meyer and Allen distinguished affective commitment (wanting to stay), continuance commitment (needing to stay because leaving is costly) and normative commitment (feeling obliged to stay).

    Employee experience (EX) is the sum of an employee’s interactions with the organisation across the employee lifecycle, from recruitment to exit, and how the employee perceives them.

    Employee well-being is the physical, mental, financial and social health of employees, in so far as work affects it and the employer can support it.

    How the concepts relate

    Engagement Satisfaction Employee experience Well-being
    Core questionWill employees give extra effort and stay?Are employees content?What is it like to work here at each stage?Are employees healthy and able to sustain their work?
    NatureActive, motivationalPassive, attitudinalA design of processes and interactionsA condition
    Typical measuresEngagement index, eNPS, Q12Satisfaction surveysLifecycle and moment surveys, HR service dataAbsenteeism, burnout scales, use of support services
    Typical driversManager, growth, meaningful work, recognitionPay, conditions, policiesOnboarding, tools, processes, managersWorkload, flexibility, support, financial security

    The concepts connect: good employee experience and well-being make engagement possible, and engagement is the outcome most closely linked to performance. Herzberg’s two-factor theory (Chapter 7) explains why satisfaction and engagement differ: hygiene factors such as pay and policies prevent dissatisfaction, while motivators such as achievement, recognition and growth create engagement.

    Why engagement matters

    Gallup’s meta-analyses of business units across many organisations consistently report that units in the top quartile of engagement outperform those in the bottom quartile on profitability, productivity, customer ratings, absenteeism, safety incidents and turnover. These are correlations and the causality runs both ways (successful units also make people more engaged), but the consistency of the finding across industries is the main evidence that engagement is a business issue and not only an HR one. Gallup’s State of the Global Workplace 2025 reported that only 21% of employees worldwide were engaged, down from 23%, driven largely by a fall in engagement among managers.

    5.2Engagement models

    In short

    The main models describe engagement as an outcome (Aon), a set of workplace conditions (Gallup Q12), a psychological state with preconditions (Kahn) or a balance of demands and resources (JD-R).

    Recall

    • Aon: Say, Stay, Strive
    • Gallup Q12: hierarchy of needs at work
    • Kahn: meaningfulness, safety, availability
    • JD-R: demands → burnout; resources → engagement

    Aon: Say, Stay, Strive

    Aon (formerly Aon Hewitt) defines engagement through three observable outcomes. Engaged employees Say: speak positively about the organisation to colleagues, potential employees and customers. They Stay: have an intense desire to remain with the organisation. They Strive: are motivated to exert extra effort for the organisation’s success. Aon’s survey measures these with six items (two per outcome) and links them to engagement drivers, grouped into areas such as the work itself, people, opportunities, total rewards, company practices and quality of life. The analytical value of the model lies in identifying which drivers most influence engagement in a particular organisation.

    Gallup Q12

    Gallup measures engagement through twelve survey items that describe the workplace conditions engaged employees report. The items are arranged as a hierarchy of needs at work:

    Level Theme of the items
    Basic needsKnowing what is expected; having the materials and equipment to do the work
    Individual contributionOpportunity to do what one does best; recent recognition; someone at work who cares; encouragement of development
    TeamworkOpinions count; connection to the organisation’s mission; co-workers committed to quality; a close friend at work
    GrowthRecent conversation about progress; opportunities to learn and grow

    The first item, about knowing what is expected at work, sits at the base: if expectations are unclear, other engagement efforts have little effect. Most items describe things a manager controls, which is why Gallup attributes about 70% of the variance in team engagement to the manager.

    Kahn’s conditions for engagement

    Kahn identified three psychological conditions that determine whether people engage: meaningfulness (the work feels worthwhile), psychological safety (people can show themselves without fear of negative consequences; Chapter 7) and availability (people have the physical, emotional and psychological resources to engage). Interventions can be mapped to each condition: job design for meaningfulness, leadership behaviour for safety, workload and well-being for availability.

    Job demands–resources (JD-R) model

    Developed by Arnold Bakker and Evangelia Demerouti, the JD-R model divides job characteristics into demands (workload, time pressure, emotional and physical strain) and resources (autonomy, support, feedback, development opportunities). High demands drain energy and lead to burnout; resources fuel engagement and buffer the effect of demands. Its practical implication is that engagement and burnout are managed through the same two levers: reduce excessive demands, increase resources.

    5.3What drives engagement

    In short

    The strongest drivers are the immediate manager, meaningful work, growth, recognition, fairness and confidence in leadership. Perks rarely appear among the top drivers.

    Recall

    • Manager (≈70% of variance)
    • Job characteristics model
    • Growth and career
    • Recognition
    • Fairness (equity theory)
    • Leadership and purpose
    Driver Why it matters Practical levers
    Immediate managerSets expectations, gives feedback, recognises, supports growthManager selection, training and accountability for team engagement
    Meaningful workHackman and Oldham’s job characteristics (skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback) increase motivationJob enrichment, autonomy, line of sight to customers and results
    Growth and careerLearning and visible progression keep people engaged and reduce exitsCareer paths, internal mobility, development plans
    RecognitionTimely, specific recognition reinforces behaviour and signals valuePeer and manager recognition programmes; spot awards
    FairnessPerceived unfairness in pay, promotion or treatment reduces effort (equity theory)Transparent pay ranges, calibrated ratings, consistent policies
    Leadership and purposeConfidence in direction and in leaders’ integrityLeader communication, consistency between stated values and decisions
    Well-being and flexibilityAvailability of energy (Kahn; JD-R)Workload management, flexible work, support services

    Perks are not drivers. Free food, offsites and recreation rooms may raise satisfaction briefly but rarely appear among the top drivers in driver analyses. In an interview, starting an engagement answer with perks signals a shallow understanding.

    5.4Measuring engagement and experience

    In short

    Combine a periodic survey with pulses, lifecycle surveys, interviews and passive data. Report at team level only above an anonymity threshold, and always pair measurement with action.

    Recall

    • Census survey; driver analysis
    • Pulse surveys
    • eNPS = %promoters − %detractors
    • Stay and exit interviews
    • Passive data
    • Anonymity threshold

    Methods

    Method Description Strengths Watch-outs
    Annual census surveyAll employees; 30–60 items on engagement outcomes and drivers; benchmarkedComplete picture; driver analysisLong; results arrive late; fatigue if no action follows
    Pulse surveys5–15 items, monthly or quarterlyTrend tracking; tests whether actions workOver-surveying reduces response rates
    Lifecycle (moment) surveysAt set points: 30 and 90 days after joining, after promotion, after return from leave, at exitDiagnoses specific stages of EXRequires HRIS triggers
    eNPS“How likely are you to recommend this organisation as a place to work?” on 0–10Simple, comparable over timeOne number with no explanation; pair with a comment question
    Stay interviewsStructured conversations with valued employees about why they stay and what might make them leavePrevents regretted exitsMust lead to visible action
    Exit interviews and surveysReasons for leavingIdentifies avoidable exitsLeavers soften reasons; a survey a few months after exit is more candid
    Passive dataAttrition, absenteeism, overtime, leave not taken, internal mobilityObjective, continuousPrivacy and data-protection obligations (Chapter 9)
    AI-based listeningConversational bots (e.g., Amber by inFeedo, an Indian platform), text analytics of commentsScale; early warningTrust and surveillance concerns

    eNPS

    Respondents scoring 9–10 are promoters, 7–8 passives and 0–6 detractors. eNPS = % promoters − % detractors, giving a score between −100 and +100.

    Example: 200 responses, 70 promoters (35%), 80 passives (40%), 50 detractors (25%): eNPS = 35 − 25 = +10. Track the trend and compare segments rather than interpreting the absolute number.

    Survey design essentials

    • Anonymity threshold: results are reported only for groups with at least a minimum number of respondents (commonly 5–10), so individuals cannot be identified.
    • Driver analysis: statistical analysis (correlation or regression of engagement outcomes on driver items) shows which drivers matter most. Priorities are drivers that are high in impact and low in score.
    • Response rate: low response rates bias results towards the most or least satisfied; 70% or more is a common target for census surveys.
    • Language and access: for shop-floor workforces, surveys in local languages through kiosks, paper or voice formats, with facilitation.

    5.5From results to action

    In short

    Surveys build engagement only when results are shared quickly and acted on. Managers own a few team-level actions; HR provides data and support.

    Recall

    • Share within 4–6 weeks
    • 2–3 priorities per team
    • Manager-owned action plans
    • “You said, we did”
    • Re-measure with pulses
    1. Share results quickly, within four to six weeks, at company, function and team level.
    2. Choose two or three priorities per team, based on driver analysis and team discussion, rather than trying to fix everything.
    3. Managers own team action plans, developed with their teams; HR supplies data, toolkits and coaching. Company-wide issues (pay, policy, leadership) are owned centrally.
    4. Communicate actions visibly, linking them to what employees said (“you said, we did”).
    5. Re-measure with pulse surveys to check whether actions are working.

    Why surveys fail. The most common reason is no visible action: employees conclude that answering is pointless, response rates fall and cynicism rises. Other causes are action plans owned only by HR, too many priorities, and results that are used to blame managers rather than support them.

    5.6Attrition and retention

    In short

    Attrition must be segmented before it is interpreted: voluntary or involuntary, regretted or not, by tenure, role and manager. Retention strategies target the specific drivers found.

    Recall

    • Attrition formula
    • Voluntary vs involuntary
    • Regretted vs non-regretted
    • Early attrition
    • India: 17.7% (2024)
    • Cost of turnover
    • Retention levers

    Measurement

    Measure Formula or definition
    Annual attrition rateSeparations during the year ÷ average headcount during the year × 100
    Annualised monthly rateMonthly separations ÷ average monthly headcount × 12 (approximation)
    Voluntary attritionResignations initiated by employees
    Involuntary attritionExits initiated by the employer (terminations, retrenchment)
    Regretted attritionVoluntary exits of employees the organisation wanted to keep (good performers, critical skills)
    Early attritionHires leaving within 90 or 365 days ÷ hires in that cohort
    Retention rateEmployees present at both the start and end of a period ÷ employees at the start

    India contextAon’s India survey reported overall attrition of 17.7% in 2024, down from 18.7% in 2023, as attrition returned towards pre-pandemic levels. Rates vary widely: frontline roles in retail, quick commerce, BPO and delivery often exceed 50% a year, while public-sector companies and core manufacturing officer cadres are much lower. Always segment before comparing.

    Cost of turnover

    Turnover costs include separation costs (exit processing, notice-period disruption), vacancy costs (lost output, overtime), replacement costs (recruitment, agency fees, interview time) and ramp-up costs (training and the months before the new hire reaches full productivity). Estimates commonly range from several months’ salary for junior roles to more than a year’s salary for senior or specialised roles.

    Diagnosing and acting on attrition

    Diagnostic question Data Possible action
    Is it concentrated under particular managers?Attrition by managerManager coaching or change
    Is it in the first year?Early attrition by cohort and sourceSelection and onboarding improvements
    Is pay below market for leavers?Compa-ratios of leavers vs stayers; offer dataTargeted market correction
    Are people leaving for growth?Exit reasons; time since last promotion or moveCareer paths, internal mobility
    Is it linked to shifts, commute or workload?Attendance, overtime, location dataRoster, transport and workload changes

    Retention levers therefore vary: compensation corrections, manager quality, career and internal mobility, flexibility, recognition, onboarding and, for critical individuals, retention bonuses or long-term incentives. Stay interviews identify risks before resignation.

    5.8Well-being and burnout

    In short

    Well-being covers physical, mental, financial and social health. Burnout is an occupational phenomenon caused by chronic, unmanaged workplace stress, so the most effective interventions change work, not only individuals.

    Recall

    • Four dimensions
    • Burnout (WHO ICD-11, 2019)
    • Interventions: organisation, team, individual
    • Mental Healthcare Act 2017
    • Right to disconnect (not law in India)
    • Menstrual leave (Karnataka 2025)

    Dimensions

    Dimension Examples of employer support
    PhysicalHealth insurance, health check-ups, ergonomic workplaces, safety programmes
    Mental and emotionalEmployee assistance programmes (counselling), mental-health training for managers, manageable workloads
    FinancialFair and timely pay, financial-literacy programmes, salary advances, retirement benefits
    SocialInclusion, team connection, family-friendly policies

    Burnout

    The World Health Organization’s ICD-11 (in force since 2022, adopted in 2019) describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition, resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It has three dimensions: exhaustion, mental distance from or cynicism about the job, and reduced professional efficacy. Because its cause lies in work, the most effective responses change work.

    Level Interventions Effectiveness
    OrganisationStaffing and workload levels, realistic deadlines, manager training, flexible work, leave that is actually taken, protected time without meetingsHighest: addresses causes
    TeamNorms on after-hours communication, psychological safety, rotation of on-call duties, recognitionModerate to high
    IndividualCounselling (EAP), resilience and stress-management training, mental-health first aidersSupportive, but cannot offset excessive demands

    India context

    • Working hours debate. The death of a 26-year-old EY employee in Pune in July 2024, and her mother’s letter about workload, started a national discussion on overwork and a government inquiry. Comments by business leaders advocating 70- and 90-hour working weeks (2023, 2025) kept the topic in public debate. The OSH Code limits working weeks to 48 hours, with overtime at twice the normal rate and capped at 144 hours a quarter under the central rules.
    • Mental health. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 requires insurers to cover mental illness on the same basis as physical illness; the insurance regulator directed insurers to comply in 2018.
    • Right to disconnect. Law in France (2017), Portugal (2021) and Australia (2024). In India it exists only as private member’s bills introduced in Parliament since 2018, not as law. Some Indian employers set their own norms.
    • Menstrual leave. Karnataka’s 2025 policy grants women one paid day of menstrual leave a month (12 a year) across government and private establishments, without a medical certificate; some companies, such as Zomato, introduced such leave voluntarily earlier.
    • Health check-ups. The central OSH rules require free annual medical check-ups for workers aged 40 and above in docks, mines and construction; states define requirements for other establishments.

    5.9Employee experience (EX)

    In short

    EX design applies customer-experience methods to the employee lifecycle: personas, journey maps and moments that matter, then fixes the friction found.

    Recall

    • Lifecycle stages
    • Personas
    • Journey mapping
    • Moments that matter
    • Digital EX and AI

    Lifecycle stages: attract, recruit, onboard, develop, perform, retain, separate, and alumni.

    EX design methods, borrowed from customer experience:

    • Personas: representative employee profiles with distinct needs, for example a first-year engineering trainee at a remote plant, a working parent in a hybrid sales role, or a dark-store shift lead.
    • Journey maps: the steps a persona goes through in a process (such as onboarding), with what they do, think and feel at each step, and where friction occurs.
    • Moments that matter: points with outsized influence on how employees feel about the organisation, such as the first day, the first one-to-one with a manager, a missed promotion, return from maternity leave, relocation and resignation.

    Typical findings and fixes. Common friction points include delayed laptops and system access on day one, reimbursements that take weeks, HR queries passed between teams, and managers who are unprepared for new joiners. Fixes combine process redesign, digital tools (employee self-service, chatbots, HRMS workflows) and manager preparation.

    AI in EX. AI assistants answer HR queries, recommend learning, summarise feedback and predict attrition risk. IBM, for example, has described using AI to predict employees likely to leave. The risks are algorithmic bias, loss of human contact in sensitive matters and perceptions of surveillance; HR should keep humans responsible for sensitive conversations and decisions.

    5.10Employee value proposition (EVP)

    In short

    The EVP is what an organisation offers employees in return for their contribution. The employer brand is how the market perceives the organisation as an employer. A credible brand depends on a true EVP.

    Recall

    • EVP definition
    • Gartner: Opportunity, People, Organisation, Work, Rewards
    • EVP vs employer brand
    • True, distinctive, relevant, segmented
    • Build in five steps
    • Measures

    Definition. The employee value proposition is the set of rewards, opportunities and experiences an employer offers in return for employees’ skills, effort and commitment. It is the internal promise that underpins attraction and retention.

    Gartner’s EVP attributes group what employees value into five categories:

    Attribute Includes
    OpportunityCareer growth, development, internal mobility
    PeopleManager quality, colleagues, culture
    OrganisationReputation, purpose, stability, social responsibility
    WorkInteresting work, autonomy, flexibility, work-life balance
    RewardsPay, benefits, recognition

    EVP and employer brand

    EVP Employer brand
    NatureThe actual offer and promiseThe perception held by candidates and the market
    AudienceMainly current employeesMainly potential employees and the public
    Built throughPolicies, practices, leadership and managersCommunication, campus presence, social media, employee advocacy, review sites
    TestIs it true for employees?Is it consistent with what employees say publicly?
    ExampleHCLTech’s “Employees First, Customers Second” philosophy (Vineet Nayar, from 2005): inverting the pyramid, open feedback on managers, service tickets employees can raise against enabling functionsHCLTech’s resulting reputation as an employee-centric IT employer

    Employees publish their experience on Glassdoor, AmbitionBox and LinkedIn, so an employer brand that is not supported by the EVP is quickly contradicted.

    Building an EVP

    1. Research with current employees (surveys, focus groups, exit and stay data), candidates and competitors’ offers.
    2. Identify what is true and distinctive. Generic claims such as “great culture and great people” differentiate nothing; specific claims (such as cross-business exposure at ITC, or township living and long careers at a manufacturing major) do.
    3. Segment. Define a core EVP with variations for key groups, for example frontline staff, engineers, sales and corporate functions, whose priorities differ.
    4. Test and launch internally first, so employees recognise the EVP as accurate before it is communicated externally.
    5. Measure: offer acceptance rate, applicant quality, referral rate, eNPS and attrition in target segments.

    Indian employers often cited

    Tata Steel introduced the 8-hour working day (1912), leave with pay (1920) and maternity benefit (1928) before Indian law required them, and is known for township-based welfare. HCLTech is the standard example of an EVP built on an explicit management philosophy. Companies such as Marico, HUL and Infosys appear regularly in best-employer rankings; treat rankings as indicators rather than proof of a strong EVP.

    5.11Interview questions

    In short

    Start with diagnosis by segment, name the manager as the main lever, and end with how you would measure the result.

    What is the difference between engagement and satisfaction?

    Satisfaction is contentment with the job and its conditions; engagement is active commitment that shows in discretionary effort. A well-paid employee in an easy role can be satisfied but disengaged. Herzberg’s theory explains the difference: pay and conditions prevent dissatisfaction, while achievement, recognition and growth create engagement.

    Attrition in a business unit is 28%. How would you approach it?

    First segment it: voluntary or involuntary, regretted or not, by role, tenure, location and manager. Then diagnose using exit and stay data, compa-ratios of leavers, early-attrition patterns and engagement results for the unit. Act on the two or three main causes found, such as a market correction for critical roles, manager coaching, career paths or onboarding changes, and track regretted attrition quarterly.

    Engagement scores are high, but so is attrition. How can both be true?

    The people leaving may not have responded to the survey, or may belong to groups the survey misses, such as contract staff or first-year hires. Pay may be uncompetitive, so people like working here but leave for much higher offers. Or the survey may measure satisfaction rather than intention to stay. Compare responses and exits by segment, and examine exit reasons and pay data.

    How would you build an EVP for an organisation?

    Research what current employees value and how the organisation compares with competitors; identify attributes that are both true and distinctive, using Gartner’s five categories; segment the EVP for key groups; test it internally before communicating it externally; and measure its effect on offer acceptance, applicant quality, referrals and attrition.

    How would you engage workers in a unionised cement plant?

    Focus on the drivers that matter on the shop floor: safety, fair treatment by supervisors, predictable shifts, facilities such as canteen and transport, family welfare and skill growth. Listen through local-language surveys and face-to-face forums, involve the union rather than bypassing it, and make line supervisors accountable for engagement with training and support.
